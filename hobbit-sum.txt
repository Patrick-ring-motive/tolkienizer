A Detailed Summary of 'An Unexpected Party'

Introduction: A Hobbit's Quiet Life

In the quiet, respectable heart of the Shire lives a hobbit named Bilbo Baggins, the very embodiment of a life dedicated to comfort, predictability, and the simple, tangible pleasures of good food and a warm hearth. His world, centered around his luxurious hobbit-hole, Bag End, is a meticulously ordered one, free from the jarring and inconvenient disruptions of adventure. This peaceful baseline is the story's gentle beginning, a portrait of tranquility so complete that its imminent shattering feels all the more momentous. Within Mr. Baggins, however, lies a subtle but significant tension. He is a Baggins, solid and sensible, yet he is also the son of the famous Belladonna Took, and from this maternal line flows a latent spirit of curiosity and daring that has long remained dormant. This internal conflict between his comfortable Baggins nature and his adventurous Took heritage is the quiet hum beneath the surface of his placid life—a life that is about to be irrevocably and unexpectedly changed.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------


1. The Inhabitant of Bag End

1.1. A Description of a Hobbit-Hole

In a hole in the ground there lived a hobbit. Yet this was not, as the telling might suggest, "a nasty, dirty, wet hole, filled with the ends of worms and an oozy smell." On the contrary, it was a hobbit-hole, and that means comfort.

Known as Bag End, Bilbo Baggins’s dwelling was a place of meticulous order and cozy luxury. Its entrance was a perfectly round, green-painted door like a porthole, with a shiny yellow brass knob set in the exact middle. This door opened onto a tube-shaped hall, a most comfortable tunnel with panelled walls, tiled and carpeted floors, and an abundance of polished chairs. There were also "lots and lots of pegs for hats and coats—the hobbit was fond of visitors." From this main passage, which wound deep into the side of The Hill, many little round doors opened into the home's various rooms. Bedrooms, bathrooms, pantries, kitchens, dining-rooms, and even "whole rooms devoted to clothes," were all conveniently situated on the same floor. The finest of these rooms were on the left-hand side, for they alone had deep-set round windows that looked out over the hobbit's garden and the meadows sloping down to the river. It was a home that perfectly reflected the nature of its owner: secure, comfortable, and well-ordered.

1.2. The Character of Bilbo Baggins

This particular hobbit was Mr. Bilbo Baggins, a "very well-to-do hobbit" of impeccable respectability. The Bagginses of The Hill were known for being rich and predictable; they "never had any adventures or did anything unexpected." You could, with absolute certainty, know what a Baggins would say on any matter without ever needing to ask.

This stolid predictability, however, was only half of Bilbo's story. His mother was the renowned Belladonna Took, whose family was richer than the Bagginses but decidedly less respectable. The Tooks were marked by an unusual, almost un-hobbitlike quality; it was rumored a distant ancestor had taken a fairy wife, and from time to time, members of the Took clan would simply go off on adventures. When this happened, "They discreetly disappeared, and the family hushed it up." While Belladonna herself settled into a comfortable life after marrying Bungo Baggins, she passed something of this heritage to her only son. Though Bilbo looked and behaved exactly like his respectable father, there was "something a bit queer in his make-up from the Took side, something that only waited for a chance to come out." For fifty years, that chance never came, until a curious meeting one morning with the very catalyst who would provide it.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------


2. The Arrival of Gandalf

The arrival of the wizard Gandalf is not merely a chance encounter; it is the deliberate introduction of chaos into Bilbo's orderly world. This meeting serves as the story's inciting incident, presenting a direct challenge to the hobbit's deep-seated desire for a peaceful, predictable life and setting in motion the grand events that will follow.

2.1. The Morning Encounter

On a beautiful morning, as Bilbo Baggins stood at his door smoking an enormous wooden pipe, he was greeted by an old man with a staff. The stranger wore a tall pointed blue hat, a long grey cloak, a silver scarf, and immense black boots. A long white beard hung down below his waist. When Bilbo wished him a cheerful "Good Morning!", the old man, Gandalf, immediately probed the hobbit's meaning with a series of witty questions.

"What do you mean?" he said. "Do you wish me a good morning, or mean that it is a good morning whether I want it or not; or that you feel good this morning; or that it is a morning to be good on?"

Bilbo, slightly flustered but polite, replied, "All of them at once," and invited the stranger to sit and share a pipe. After Bilbo blew a particularly beautiful smoke-ring, Gandalf declared he had no time for such pleasantries. The hobbit, having decided the old man was "not quite his sort," tried to dismiss him with a firm, final "Good morning!" Gandalf, however, was not so easily turned away. "What a lot of things you do use Good morning for!" he said. "Now you mean that you want to get rid of me, and that it won’t be good till I move off."

"Not at all, not at all, my dear sir!" a flustered Bilbo replied. "Let me see, I don’t think I know your name?"

"Yes, yes, my dear sir—and I do know your name, Mr. Bilbo Baggins. And you do know my name, though you don’t remember that I belong to it. I am Gandalf, and Gandalf means me! To think that I should have lived to be good-morninged by Belladonna Took’s son, as if I was selling buttons at the door!"

At the mention of his mother and the wizard’s famous name, Bilbo’s composure completely crumbled, replaced by a flood of childhood memories of magic fireworks and thrilling tales of dragons and goblins. This brief exchange perfectly captures their dynamic: Bilbo's conventional, pleasant nature brushing up against Gandalf's more profound and mysterious one.

2.2. The Offer of Adventure and Its Refusal

Gandalf wasted no time, declaring, "I am looking for someone to share in an adventure that I am arranging." Bilbo’s reaction was one of immediate horror. He rejected the notion entirely, proclaiming adventures to be:

"Nasty disturbing uncomfortable things! Make you late for dinner! I can’t think what anybody sees in them."

Attempting to end the conversation, the utterly flustered hobbit tried to dismiss the wizard but, in his confusion, invited him to tea the next day—an offer he instantly regretted. As Bilbo scuttled back inside his hobbit-hole, Gandalf remained outside, laughing quietly. Before departing, he stepped up to the hobbit's beautiful green door and, with the spike of his staff, scratched a "queer sign" upon it. This subtle act was a mark of things to come, a quiet promise that despite Bilbo's refusal, adventure was now set upon its course.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------


3. An Unexpected Gathering

The arrival of the dwarves is a comical yet escalating invasion that systematically dismantles Bilbo's sense of control and domestic order. This chaotic procession physically manifests the very adventure he had so vehemently rejected, bringing the outside world crashing into the sanctity of Bag End.

3.1. The Procession of Dwarves

The next day, just before tea-time, a tremendous ring of the bell announced not Gandalf, but the first of thirteen dwarves. Dwalin pushed inside as if expected, announcing himself with a bow: "Dwalin at your service!" A bewildered Bilbo, too surprised to object, soon found his hospitality tested again by an even louder ring. This time it was Balin.

They were followed shortly by two more, Kili and Fili, who cheerfully announced their intention to "join the throng!" The word "throng" filled Mr. Baggins with dread. His bell rang again, "louder than ever," heralding the arrival of five more dwarves: Dori, Nori, Ori, Oin, and Gloin. The hobbit’s hall was now crowded with hoods of various colors. The final arrivals were announced not by a ring, but a hard knock from a stick. Bilbo pulled open the door to find four more dwarves—Bifur, Bofur, Bombur, and the "enormously important" Thorin Oakenshield—falling in a heap on his mat, with Gandalf leaning on his staff and laughing behind them. The wizard had not only made a dent on the door, but had also knocked out the secret mark he had placed there the day before.

3.2. A Hobbit's Hospitality Overwhelmed

The dwarves immediately made themselves at home, calling for ale, porter, coffee, red wine, and a bewildering array of food. Balin requested "seed-cake," Bifur called for "raspberry jam and apple-tart," Bofur for "mince-pies and cheese," and Bombur for "pork-pie and salad." As Bilbo stumped off to the pantries, Gandalf called after him, "Put on a few eggs, there’s a good fellow! And just bring out the cold chicken and pickles!"

Bilbo, "positively flummoxed," found his pantries raided and his peaceful routine utterly shattered, muttering aloud, "Confusticate and bebother these dwarves!"

After the feast, the dwarves astonished Bilbo further by clearing up with remarkable speed and dexterity. To the hobbit's squeaking fright, they balanced columns of plates and sang a boisterous song:

Chip the glasses and crack the plates!
Blunt the knives and bend the forks!
That’s what Bilbo Baggins hates–
Smash the bottles and burn the corks!

Cut the cloth and tread on the fat!
Pour the milk on the pantry floor!
Leave the bones on the bedroom mat!
Splash the wine on every door!

Dump the crocks in a boiling bowl;
Pound them up with a thumping pole;
And when you’ve finished, if any are whole,
Send them down the hall to roll!

That’s what Bilbo Baggins hates!
So, carefully! carefully with the plates!

Yet, "of course they did none of these dreadful things," and everything was put away "safe as quick as lightning." As the chaotic energy of the party gave way to pipe-smoke and a quieter mood, the true and far more serious purpose of their visit was about to be revealed.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------


4. The Stirring of Adventure

With the meal concluded and the domestic chaos momentarily settled, the tone of the gathering shifts dramatically. This section marks a critical turn from the comical invasion of Bilbo's home to the epic and sorrowful history of the dwarves, a tale that begins to resonate deeply with the hobbit's long-dormant Tookish spirit.

4.1. Music and a Melancholy Song

At Thorin's call, the dwarves produced their instruments: Kili and Fili brought fiddles; Dori, Nori, and Ori had flutes; Bombur a drum; Bifur and Bofur clarinets; and Dwalin and Balin brought viols. Finally, Thorin himself took up a beautiful golden harp. The music that followed was so sudden and sweet that Bilbo "forgot everything else, and was swept away into dark lands under strange moons."

As they played, the dwarves began to sing a deep-throated, melancholy song of their ancient home.

Far over the misty mountains cold
To dungeons deep and caverns old
We must away ere break of day
To seek the pale enchanted gold.

The dwarves of yore made mighty spells,
While hammers fell like ringing bells
In places deep, where dark things sleep,
In hollow halls beneath the fells.

For ancient king and elvish lord
There many a gleaming golden hoard
They shaped and wrought, and light they caught
To hide in gems on hilt of sword.

On silver necklaces they strung
The flowering stars, on crowns they hung
The dragon-fire, in twisted wire
They meshed the light of moon and sun.

Far over the misty mountains cold
To dungeons deep and caverns old
We must away, ere break of day,
To claim our long-forgotten gold.

Goblets they carved there for themselves
And harps of gold; where no man delves
There lay they long, and many a song
Was sung unheard by men or elves.

The pines were roaring on the height,
The winds were moaning in the night.
The fire was red, it flaming spread;
The trees like torches blazed with light.

The bells were ringing in the dale
And men looked up with faces pale;
The dragon’s ire more fierce than fire
Laid low their towers and houses frail.

The mountain smoked beneath the moon;
The dwarves, they heard the tramp of doom.
They fled their hall to dying fall
Beneath his feet, beneath the moon.

Far over the misty mountains grim
To dungeons deep and caverns dim
We must away, ere break of day,
To win our harps and gold from him!

The song serves as a powerful narrative device, introducing the central themes of the quest—lost treasure, displacement, and the great danger posed by the dragon—and transforms the boisterous guests into figures of tragic history.

4.2. The Tookish Awakening

The music and the mournful lyrics had a profound effect on Bilbo. A fierce love for beautiful, crafted things stirred within him, and "something Tookish woke up inside him." He was overcome with a sudden, startling desire "to go and see the great mountains, and hear the pine-trees and the waterfalls, and explore the caves, and wear a sword instead of a walking-stick."

This adventurous urge, however, was immediately countered by a potent wave of fear. The image of the song’s treasure was replaced by a vision of "plundering dragons settling on his quiet Hill." He shuddered and, in an instant, "was plain Mr. Baggins of Bag-End, Under-Hill, again." Though his comfortable Baggins nature momentarily won out, the seed of adventure had been firmly planted, setting the stage for the formal unveiling of the quest.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------


5. The Quest Revealed

With the evening's mood now set by the dwarves' sorrowful history, the true purpose of the gathering is finally revealed. The events that follow serve as the formal "call to adventure," where the history of the dwarves, the grand objective of their journey, and the essential tools for the quest are laid bare for the flummoxed hobbit.

5.1. Thorin's Declaration

After the music, Thorin rose to speak. He addressed Gandalf, his fellow dwarves, and "Mr. Baggins... our friend and fellow conspirator, this most excellent and audacious hobbit." Bilbo was too flummoxed by these titles to protest. Thorin spoke solemnly of their long journey, warning that it was one from which some "may never return."

5.2. A Moment of Panic

The phrase "may never return" was too much for poor Bilbo. A shriek "like the whistle of an engine" burst from him, and he collapsed onto the hearth-rug, "shaking like a jelly that was melting." He "kept on calling out 'struck by lightning, struck by lightning!' over and over again." The dwarves quickly laid him on a sofa to recover. Gandalf, unperturbed, remarked, "Excitable little fellow... Gets funny queer fits, but he is one of the best, one of the best—as fierce as a dragon in a pinch."

5.3. A Burglar's Honor is Questioned

As he was recovering, Bilbo crept to the door and overheard the dwarves discussing him. Gloin's voice was filled with doubt: "Will he do, do you think? ... one shriek like that in a moment of excitement would be enough to wake the dragon and all his relatives, and kill the lot of us... As soon as I clapped eyes on the little fellow bobbing and puffing on the mat, I had my doubts. He looks more like a grocer than a burglar!" This slight was the final catalyst. Hearing himself called a "little fellow bobbing on the mat" proved to be more than his pride could bear. His "Took side had won," and he "suddenly felt he would go without bed and breakfast to be thought fierce."

5.4. The Burglar's Acceptance

Bilbo strode into the room, interrupting them with a dignified and defiant speech. He declared that he would show them he was not "no good" and would join their quest, even if he had "to walk from here to the East of East and fight the wild Were-worms in the Last Desert." Gandalf confirmed his choice, revealing that he himself had put the sign on the door which read, "Burglar wants a good job, plenty of Excitement and reasonable Reward." He defended his selection of Mr. Baggins as the essential fourteenth man for the expedition, silencing all further argument with a decisive and prophetic declaration:

"If I say he is a Burglar, a Burglar he is, or will be when the time comes."

This affirmation cemented Bilbo's role and shifted the focus of the meeting to the logistical details of their great undertaking.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------


6. The Plan of the Mountain

With Bilbo now committed, however reluctantly, to the role of burglar, the focus of the gathering shifts from persuasion to the practical details of the quest. The dwarves, guided by Gandalf, reveal the strategic assets and historical context that make their seemingly impossible journey viable.

6.1. The Map and The Key

Gandalf spread a piece of parchment on the table—a map made by Thorin's grandfather, Thror. It showed the Lonely Mountain, with a dragon marked in red upon it. Most importantly, the wizard pointed to a rune on the West side, marking a hidden passage. "‘Five feet high the door and three may walk abreast’ say the runes," Gandalf explained, noting that the entrance was far too small for the dragon Smaug to use.

He then presented Thorin with a "small and curious key" with a long barrel and intricate wards. "Keep it safe!" Gandalf urged, and Thorin fastened it to a chain around his neck. This news, Thorin declared, made their quest look much more hopeful.

6.2. Thorin's Tale of Smaug's Ruin

When Bilbo obstinately insisted on a plain and clear explanation of the quest, Thorin recounted the tragic history of his people. He described the great prosperity of the dwarves under the Mountain in the days of his grandfather, Thror, when the merry town of Dale flourished nearby. Their wealth and fame, however, brought ruin.

Thorin, an eyewitness to the destruction, told of the sudden arrival of "a most specially greedy, strong and wicked worm called Smaug." He described how the dragon descended upon the mountain "in a spout of flame," setting the woods ablaze. The dwarves rushed from their great gate only to be met by the waiting dragon. Dale was destroyed, its warriors slain, and Smaug routed the dwarves from their halls, claiming all their vast wealth for himself. The few survivors, including Thorin, were forced into exile.

Gandalf then revealed how he came by the map and key, solving an old mystery for Thorin. He explained that he had found Thorin’s father, Thrain, "a prisoner in the dungeons of the Necromancer." Thrain, witless and wandering, had forgotten nearly everything but gave Gandalf the map and key to pass on to his son, setting the stage for this very quest.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------


7. Conclusion: A Night of Unsettled Dreams

As the unexpected party finally drew to a close, the dwarves made preparations for the night, ordering their breakfasts for the morning with a casualness that greatly annoyed their host. Bilbo, tired and overwhelmed, found beds for all of them on chairs and sofas before retiring to his own room. His earlier adventurousness, however, was already fading. The "Tookishness was wearing off, and he was not now quite so sure that he was going on any journey in the morning." He fell asleep to the sound of Thorin humming their melancholy song in the next room: "Far over the misty mountains cold." The tune lingered in his mind, giving him "very uncomfortable dreams" and leaving the great question of his participation hanging in the balance, as uncertain as the coming dawn.


A Detailed Summary of "Roast Mutton"

1. The Perils of the Road

The company’s journey eastward marked a profound shift, not only in landscape but in spirit. The comfortable, respectable hobbit-lands with their good roads and decent folk gave way to the sparsely populated Lone-lands, where the roads grew worse and the hills rose like dark, dreary sentinels. Old castles, bearing an evil look, crowned some of these hills, casting a gloom over the travellers that was only deepened by a sudden turn in the weather. The fine May days were supplanted by a cold, persistent rain that soaked their spirits as thoroughly as their cloaks. This descent into a more perilous world was made all the more daunting by the sudden and unexplained absence of Gandalf. For so long the company's guide and shield, his departure left them feeling rudderless and exposed just as their need for a wizard was becoming most acute.

As they trudged through the muddy tracks of the Lone-lands, the mood of the company soured with the weather. Bilbo Baggins, his hood dripping into his eyes and his pony stumbling beneath him, found himself overcome with regret, his thoughts drifting back to the simple comforts of his hobbit-hole. "I wish I was at home in my nice hole by the fire, with the kettle just beginning to sing!" he lamented internally. The dwarves, too grumpy to speak, simply jogged on, their faces grim. Their misfortunes compounded quickly. As dusk settled, they went down into a deep valley where a wind got up, and the willows along the river banks bent and sighed, adding to the gloom. Here, Oin and Gloin, despite being specialists in the craft, failed to light a fire. Then, a pony, startled by nothing, bolted and plunged into the river, taking with it the bulk of their food supplies. It was in this moment of collective misery that they finally noticed Gandalf was gone. "Just when a wizard would have been most useful, too," groaned Dori and Nori.

Glum, wet, and with mighty little for supper, their situation seemed desperate. As Oin and Gloin continued to quarrel over their failed fire, a small, unexpected event pierced their despair. Balin, ever the look-out, spotted a reddish, comfortable-looking light twinkling in the distance, shining from a wooded hill and offering the faintest glimmer of hope in the deepening dark.

2. The Burglar's First Test

The distant fire, a potential beacon of salvation, also presented the first true test of Bilbo Baggins's professional capacity. Thorin and the dwarves, having argued the risks, decided that their designated "burglar" must be the one to investigate. Upon Bilbo's stealth and reconnaissance rested the company's hopes for a hot meal and a dry place to sleep. The mission was clear: "You must go on and find out all about that light, and what it is for, and if all is perfectly safe and canny." With the dwarves' instructions to hoot like an owl if he ran into trouble, a skill he did not possess, the hobbit set off alone into the woods.

Fortunately, hobbits can move with an almost supernatural quietness, and Bilbo crept towards the light without stirring so much as a whisker on a weasel. He soon discovered the source of the glow: a very large fire of beech-logs, around which sat three very large persons. A fine, toothsome smell of roasting mutton hung in the air. Their great heavy faces, immense size, and crude language—which was "not drawing-room fashion at all"—left no doubt as to their identity. They were trolls. Their names, he would learn, were William, Bert, and Tom, and they were complaining loudly about their dinner. “Mutton yesterday, mutton today, and blimey, if it don’t look like mutton again tomorrer,” grumbled one. A second complained, “Never a blinking bit of manflesh have we had for long enough,” and griped about the drink running short. Their conversation made clear that they would happily try "roasted dwarf, or even pony, for a change."

Alarmed and disgusted, Bilbo knew he should return to warn his friends. Yet, a peculiar sense of pride and a reluctance to return empty-handed stayed his feet. He recalled tales of legendary burglars and decided that picking the trolls' pockets was the least dangerous of the heroic options. Plucking up his courage, he crept behind William and slipped his hand into an enormous pocket, pulling out a purse as large as a bag to him. It was a triumphant beginning, but it was immediately spoiled. The purse, being a troll's purse, was mischievous and had a voice of its own. "’Ere, ’oo are you?" it squeaked, and in an instant, William spun around and grabbed the terrified hobbit by the neck.

Caught fast in the troll's grip, Bilbo found his first burglarious test had ended in abject failure. Shaking all over, he was now at the mercy of three very surprised, very suspicious, and very hungry creatures, while the dwarves waited in the shadows for a signal that would never come.

3. From Bad to Worse: The Capture of the Dwarves

Bilbo's capture set off a calamitous chain reaction that swiftly doomed the rest of the company. Lacking Gandalf’s wisdom, the dwarves abandoned their strategic patience. Hearing noises from the trolls' camp but receiving no signal from Bilbo, they decided to investigate. Instead of advancing as a unified group, which might have given them a fighting chance, they crept forward one by one, a fatal error in judgment that played directly into the trolls' hands and highlighted their utter vulnerability without their guide.

The capture of the dwarves was as swift as it was systematic. Balin, the first to investigate, walked straight into the firelight expecting to find his friend, only to be promptly snatched as a nasty, smelly sack was thrown over his head. The others followed at intervals, each succumbing to the same fate. As each dwarf came up and looked in surprise at the spilled jugs and gnawed mutton, pop! went another nasty, smelly sack over his head, and he was added to the growing pile. Dwalin was captured, then Fili and Kili together, followed by Dori, Nori, and Ori in a heap, and soon all but one of their number lay helpless by the fire.

Thorin arrived last, and he was not caught unawares. Seeing the signs of a struggle and his friends' legs sticking out of sacks, he came forward expecting mischief. He leaped towards the fire, seizing a large, burning branch and thrusting it into Bert's eye, briefly putting the troll out of the fight. Bilbo, forgotten in the chaos, did his best by grabbing Tom's leg, but was kicked into a bush for his troubles. The fight, however valiant, was short-lived. Just as Tom lost a tooth to Thorin's branch, William came up from behind and popped a sack over the dwarf-leader's head, and the battle was over.

The company's situation was now utterly hopeless. All thirteen dwarves were neatly tied up in sacks, bruised and terrified, lying at the mercy of three angry trolls who were already arguing about the best way to cook them.

4. A Dawn of Stone

The trolls’ greatest weakness proved to be not their strength, but their slow-witted and argumentative nature. As they debated the fate of their captives, their bickering became a fatal vulnerability, one that was cleverly and strategically exploited from the shadows. Their quarrel, which should have been a brief prelude to a grim meal, instead became the very instrument of their undoing.

The argument began at once. Should they roast the dwarves slowly? Mince them fine and boil them? Or simply sit on them one by one and squash them into jelly? As they squabbled, their foul tempers rising, a new voice inserted itself into the fray. "No good roasting ’em now, it’d take all night," said a voice that Bert thought was William's. "Who's a-arguing?" William replied, thinking Bert had spoken. The mysterious voice, which belonged to the returned Gandalf, masterfully played them against one another, mimicking each troll in turn and stoking the flames of their disagreement. He even set them squabbling over trivialities. "The one with the yellow stockings," said Bert. "Nonsense, the one with the grey stockings," a voice like William’s replied, skillfully prolonging the argument and wasting precious time as the sky began to lighten in the east.

As the first hint of morning appeared over the hill, the trolls, still arguing heatedly, remained oblivious to their impending doom. It was then that the wizard played his final, decisive trick. Adopting a voice that sounded like William's, he declared, "Dawn take you all, and be stone to you!"

The effect was instantaneous and absolute. William, who was stooping, turned to stone on the spot. Bert and Tom were caught as they looked at him, their living forms hardening into rock. Just as the trolls of legend must be underground before sunrise, so too were these three caught by the first light, transformed forever into silent, unmoving statues.

With the threat vanquished, Gandalf stepped out from behind a tree. "Excellent!" he said, and helped a torn and scratched Bilbo climb down from a thorn-bush. The wizard's voice, which had kept the trolls bickering until the dawn could make an end of them, had saved the entire company from a terrible fate.

5. The Spoils of Victory

In the aftermath of their harrowing night, the company found that the troll encounter, for all its terror, had yielded significant rewards. The immediate discovery of food and a barrel of ale was a welcome reprieve for the starving adventurers. More strategically, however, the raid on the trolls' lair armed them with crucial equipment for the journey ahead. The acquisition of fine elvish blades, weapons whose true nature would be revealed in Rivendell, was a spoil of victory that would prove vital against foes like the goblins of the Misty Mountains.

After Gandalf freed the nearly suffocated and very annoyed dwarves from their sacks, Bombur voiced their collective frustration: "Silly time to go practising pinching and pocket-picking," he said, "when what we wanted was fire and food!" Gandalf urged them to search for the trolls' hideout, and they soon found a large stone door hidden by bushes. It resisted all their efforts until Bilbo, having luckily found a largish key on the ground where the trolls had fought, provided the means of entry. Inside, the cave was filled with the grim evidence of the trolls' victims—bones on the floor and a nasty smell—but also a wealth of plunder. Amidst an untidy litter were shelves of food, pots overflowing with gold coins, and an assortment of swords.

Two blades in particular caught their eyes because of their beautiful scabbards and jewelled hilts. Gandalf and Thorin each claimed one of these fine weapons. Bilbo, for his part, took a knife in a leather sheath, which, though a mere pocket-knife for a troll, served as a respectable short sword for a hobbit. The company also carried out the pots of coins, which they buried by the river, putting a great many spells over them just in case they ever had the chance to return. They helped themselves to what food and ale they could carry.

As they rode onward toward the East, replenished and now far better armed, Thorin asked Gandalf for an account of his timely return. The wizard explained that he had gone ahead to spy out the road, where he had met two friends from Rivendell. It was they who had warned him that three trolls had come down from the mountains and were waylaying strangers. Feeling he was needed, he had looked behind, seen the distant fire, and hurried back. "Please be more careful, next time," he chided them, "or we shall never get anywhere!"
A Detailed Summary of "A Short Rest"

The company of Thorin Oakenshield, with our Mr. Baggins and the wizard Gandalf, had at last put the Lone-lands behind them. They now stood upon the very edge of the Wild, with the great Misty Mountains looming near. Before they could dare to tackle that formidable barrier, however, a most necessary stop lay ahead: a period of rest and wise counsel in the fair, hidden valley of Rivendell. But as you will soon understand, finding the Last Homely House is no simple task, for it is hidden with great care from the wider world. The journey there proved a final, wearying test of their resolve.

Their path lay not across hills or through forests, but upon a vast and featureless slope that rose slowly to meet the feet of the nearest mountain. It was a wide, silent land the colour of heather and crumbling rock, with patches and slashes of grass-green and moss-green showing where water might be. The ground would open without warning at their feet, revealing narrow valleys with steep sides, where trees grew and water ran far below. There were dark ravines they could neither leap over nor climb into, and gullies deep with waterfalls. Most treacherous of all were the bogs, some of which appeared as pleasant green places, bright with tall flowers, but which would surely swallow a pony whole and never give it back. The only guide was a faint track marked with white stones, some small and others half-swallowed by moss, making their progress a slow and frustrating business.

As the day began to fail and tea-time had long since passed, a deep anxiety settled over the travelers. With no moon yet risen, the light grew dim, and Bilbo’s pony began to stumble on roots and stones. Their search seemed fruitless, and the comforts of the Last Homely House a distant dream. Just as they came to the edge of a steep fall, so suddenly that Gandalf’s own horse nearly slipped down the slope, the wizard cried out, "Here it is at last!" Gathering at the precipice, they peered into the valley far below, preparing for the descent, unaware that the true test was not of their endurance, but of their tolerance for elvish mischief.

An Elvish Welcome

The descent into the secret valley of Rivendell began, and with it came the company’s first true encounter with the elves. For the dwarves, this was a meeting of old and not entirely friendly acquaintances; for Bilbo, it was an introduction to a folk he had only ever heard of in tales. Their welcome, full of song and teasing, stood in merry contrast to the grim determination that had marked the dwarves’ long journey from the west.

Down the steep, zig-zag path they slithered and slipped in the growing dusk. As they descended, the very air seemed to change. It grew warmer, and the heavy scent of pine-trees made Bilbo drowsy, so that he nodded and nearly bumped his nose on his pony’s neck. The trees themselves shifted from pine to beech and oak, and a comfortable feeling settled over them in the twilight. They had just reached an open glade when a burst of song, like laughter, echoed from the trees above.

O! What are you doing, And where are you going? Your ponies need shoeing! The river is flowing! O! tra-la-la-lally here down in the valley!

O! What are you seeking, And where are you making? The faggots are reeking, The bannocks are baking! O! tril-lil-lil-lolly the valley is jolly, ha! ha!

O! Where are you going With beards all a-wagging? No knowing, no knowing What brings Mister Baggins And Balin and Dwalin down into the valley in June ha! ha!

O! Will you be staying, Or will you be flying? Your ponies are straying! The daylight is dying! To fly would be folly, To stay would be jolly And listen and hark Till the end of the dark to our tune ha! ha!

These were elves, of course, and as they revealed themselves, a tall young fellow bowed to Gandalf and Thorin, saying, "Welcome to the valley! You are a little out of your way, but we will set you right. Are you going to stay a bit and sing with us, or will you go straight on?" Tired as they were, the dwarves were all for supper as soon as possible just then, and would not stay. The elves, who think dwarves foolish, began to tease them, especially about their beards, which made the dwarves gruff and annoyed. Gandalf, however, was already among them, talking merrily, while Bilbo felt a curious mix of delight and fear. He loved elves, though he seldom met them, and as they knew his name and all about him, he thought their opinion of his adventure might be interesting.

The elves guided them to a good path and at last to the brink of the river. It was a fast-flowing mountain stream, and their only way across was a narrow stone bridge with no parapet. Leading their ponies one by one, they crossed carefully while the elves, holding bright lanterns, sang a merry song. "Don’t dip your beard in the foam, father!" they cried to Thorin. "Mind Bilbo doesn’t eat all the cakes! He is too fat to get through key-holes yet!" they called. With a final "Good night!" from Gandalf, the party left the singers behind and came at last to the Last Homely House, where the doors were flung wide in welcome.

The Last Homely House

Now it is a strange thing, but things that are good to have and days that are good to spend are soon told about, and not much to listen to. Their stay in that good house was a time of perfect sanctuary, a fourteen-day rest that mended not just their bruises but their very spirits. In that valley, evil things did not come, and it stood as a bastion of peace and renewal against the harshness of the Wild.

The master of the house was Elrond. He was an elf-friend—one of those people whose fathers came into the strange stories before the beginning of History, the wars of the evil goblins and the elves and the first men in the North. He was, as the tales say, "as noble and as fair in face as an elf-lord, as strong as a warrior, as wise as a wizard, as venerable as a king of dwarves, and as kind as summer."

His was a house perfect for any soul, whether you liked food or sleep best, or work and story-telling, or a pleasant mixture of them all. Indeed, you might find whatever your heart desired there, save for evil, which could find no foothold in that blessed valley. In those few days, the company grew refreshed and strong. Their clothes were mended as well as their bruises, their tempers and their hopes were healed, and their bags were refilled with provisions light to carry but strong enough to see them over the mountain passes. Bilbo himself would gladly have stopped there for ever and ever. Yet their purpose was not forgotten, and their plans were improved with the very best advice, leading to discoveries that would prove essential to their quest.

Revelations and Ancient Lore

But as you must understand, it was not only weary limbs that were mended in that house, but the very fabric of the quest itself. The wisdom of Elrond, who knew all about runes of every kind, shed light on secrets the dwarves had carried unknowingly. These discoveries provided not only the crucial clues needed for their quest to succeed but also a deeper, more resonant history to the adventure, connecting their journey to the great wars and legends of old.

The Swords of Gondolin

Elrond’s knowledge was first applied to the swords the company had recovered from the trolls' lair. After examining them, he declared that they were not troll-make but were in fact ancient blades of the High Elves of the West, his own kin. They were famous blades, forged in the hidden city of Gondolin for the Goblin-wars many ages ago. The runes upon them named their lineage:

* Thorin's sword was Orcrist, the Goblin-cleaver.
* Gandalf's sword was Glamdring, the Foe-hammer, which the king of Gondolin himself once wore.

"Whence did the trolls get them, I wonder?" said Thorin, looking at his sword with new interest. "I could not say," Elrond answered, "but one may guess that your trolls had plundered other plunderers, or come on the remnants of old robberies in some hold in the mountains."

The Secret of the Map

Later, as a crescent moon shone in the sky, Elrond examined Thorin’s map and made an even greater discovery. "What is this?" he said, as the white moonlight shone through the parchment, revealing faint letters that could not be seen in normal light. "There are moon-letters here."

As Elrond explained, moon-letters are rune-letters that can only be seen when a moon of the same shape and in the same season as the day they were written shines behind the map. They are written with silver pens—a clever invention of the dwarves, as your friends could tell you. These, he surmised, must have been written on a midsummer’s eve under a crescent moon, a long while ago. With Gandalf and Thorin looking on, he read the secret message aloud:

"Stand by the grey stone when the thrush knocks, and the setting sun with the last light of Durin’s Day will shine upon the key-hole."

Thorin explained that Durin’s Day is the first day of the dwarves’ New Year, which falls on the first day of the last moon of Autumn on the threshold of Winter. "But this will not help us much, I fear," he said, "for it passes our skill in these days to guess when such a time will come again." And so a vital clue was found, yet it was tied to a moment in time that might be close at hand, or lost to them for years to come.

A New Beginning

On a Midsummer’s morning as fair and fresh as could be dreamed, the company prepared to depart. They had spent fourteen days in that good house, and the bruises and anxieties of the road had faded. Now, they were restored in body and spirit, and armed with both the physical and intellectual tools for the true quest: the legendary swords Orcrist and Glamdring, and the secret knowledge of how to find the hidden door into the Mountain.

And so they rode east, their ponies' heads held high and their own hearts filled with a hope that was now grounded not in wishful thinking, but in the lore of old. The Last Homely House was behind them; the great, grim shadow of the Misty Mountains lay ahead.
A Detailed Summary of Chapter Four: Over Hill and Under Hill

1.0 The Perilous Ascent into the Misty Mountains

The brief respite in the fair valley of Rivendell was over, and the company’s journey took a grim turn as they began the arduous climb into the Misty Mountains. Guided by the wise advice of Elrond and the deep knowledge of Gandalf, they took the correct road to the right pass, yet the path was hard, dangerous, and profoundly lonely. The high hopes they carried from the Last Homely House on a bright midsummer morning quickly withered, a stark prelude to the psychological descent that was to follow their physical one.

The toll of the climb was immense. The air grew bitter cold, and a shrill wind wailed among the rocks, a constant, unnerving companion. Their progress was threatened by boulders that, loosened by the midday sun, came galloping down the mountain-sides, sometimes crashing perilously close. The nights offered little comfort, being chill and unnerving; the profound silence seemed to dislike being broken, discouraging them from song or talk. Their spirits sank into a gloom as deep as the valleys below. Bilbo, shivering, found his thoughts drifting back to the warmth of his hobbit-hole and the simple joys of haymaking, a paradise lost to his present misery.

Gandalf, ever the wisest, knew how evil and danger had thrived in the Wild since the dragons had driven men from the lands and the goblins had spread in secret. He understood that even the best-laid plans of wise wizards can go astray when venturing over the Edge of the Wild, and a growing foreboding told him that they would not pass over those great, lonely mountains without some fearful adventure.

Their weary climb and quiet despair were soon to be shattered. A sudden, violent turn in the weather—far more than a simple thunderstorm—was gathering, poised to change their course and plunge them into the very heart of the peril Gandalf feared.

2.0 The Thunder-Battle and the Unsettling Shelter

The storm that descended upon them was no ordinary tempest; it was a thunder-battle of colossal proportions, a war waged between the very mountains themselves. As the company sheltered precariously under a hanging rock, storms clashed from East and West, unleashing a terrifying display of elemental power. Lightning splintered on the peaks, the rocks shivered, and great crashes of thunder split the air, rolling into every cave and hollow. Across the valley, stone-giants emerged to hurl massive rocks at one another for sport, their guffaws echoing as boulders smashed among the trees far below.

“This won’t do at all!” declared Thorin, fearing they would be blown off the path, drowned, or kicked sky-high for a football. After a grumpy argument with Gandalf, he dispatched Fili and Kili to search for a safer shelter. The young dwarves soon returned with seemingly good news: they had discovered a dry cave nearby. The party, drenched and frightened, eagerly made for this new refuge, though Gandalf was suspicious, knowing that caves in the mountains were seldom unoccupied.

In the heart of that warring mountain, the cave became an island of deceptive peace. As Gandalf wove colored smoke-rings that danced near the stony roof—a final, fleeting moment of simple magic—the dwarves' talk turned from the immediate terror to the distant gold. Lulled by the murmur of treasure and the camaraderie of their pipes, they drifted into a sleep from which they would be brutally awakened, their brief comfort a cruel prelude to the mountain's true welcome.

3.0 But mountain shelters are treacherous things, and the calm that settled within the cave was merely a mask for the horror that was about to unfold. Bilbo, unable to sleep, eventually drifted into nasty and prophetic dreams. He dreamt that a crack in the back wall grew bigger and bigger, opening wider and wider, and that the floor of the cave was giving way beneath him. He awoke with a horrible start to find his nightmare becoming reality. A crack had indeed opened, and he was just in time to see the last of the ponies' tails disappearing into it before he let out a surprisingly loud yell for a hobbit.

His cry was too late to save them but served as a crucial warning. In an instant, goblins poured from the new passage—"big goblins, great ugly-looking goblins, lots of goblins"—and seized the entire company, grabbing at least six for each dwarf and two for Bilbo, dragging them through the opening before they could resist.

Only Gandalf escaped. Bilbo’s yell had awakened the wizard in a splintered second. As the goblins lunged, a terrific flash of light—Gandalf’s own sudden magic—lit the cave, accompanied by a smell like gunpowder. Several goblins fell dead, and before the others could recover, the crack closed with a snap. The dwarves and the hobbit were prisoners on the wrong side, their protector vanished, roughly hauled away into the deep, dark, and claustrophobic passages of the goblin realm, having traded the open peril of the storm for the malevolent confinement of the mountain's heart.

4.0 The Depths of Goblin-Town

The descent into the mountain’s core was a terrifying ordeal. The company was dragged through passages that were deep, dark, and horribly stuffy, a blackness so complete that only the goblins could see. Their captors were rough, pinching them unmercifully while chuckling in their horrible, stony voices as they journeyed ever downwards. Bilbo, more miserable than ever, wished again and again for his bright hobbit-hole.

After a long journey, a glimmer of red light appeared ahead. The goblins began to croak a terrifying song, keeping time with the flap of their flat feet on the stone and shaking their prisoners with the verses.

Clap! Snap! the black crack! Grip, grab! Pinch, nab! And down down to Goblin-town You go, my lad!

Clash, crash! Crush, smash! Hammer and tongs! Knocker and gongs! Pound, pound, far underground! Ho, ho! my lad!

Swish, smack! Whip crack! Batter and beat! Yammer and bleat! Work, work! Nor dare to shirk, While Goblins quaff, and Goblins laugh, Round and round far underground Below, my lad!

The song’s meaning became painfully clear as whips were brought out, driving the company before them into a great cavern lit by a red fire and wall-torches. The cavern was filled with goblins who greeted the prisoners with cruel laughter and stamping. The company’s ponies were already huddled in a corner, their baggage being rummaged through by goblins before the animals themselves were eaten. The prisoners were chained, hands behind their backs, and linked together in a line, with little Bilbo tugging at the end. They were then dragged before a "tremendous goblin with a huge head" who sat upon a large flat stone, ready to pass judgment.

5.0 Confrontation with the Great Goblin

The interrogation began under the oppressive gaze of the Great Goblin and his guards. Goblins are cruel, wicked creatures, clever makers of malicious devices, from axes and daggers to instruments of torture. They held a special grudge against Thorin’s people, but they hated everyone and everything orderly and prosperous with equal measure. "We found them sheltering in our Front Porch," reported a goblin driver, pulling Bilbo’s chain so he fell to his knees.

The Great Goblin accused Thorin of spying and thievery. Thorin, attempting politeness, replied that they were merely sheltering from the storm on a journey to visit relatives. But Thorin’s evasions were rendered meaningless in an instant. A goblin driver held forth the captured sword, and a shock of recognition passed through the cavern. The Great Goblin knew the blade at once. It was Orcrist, the "Goblin-cleaver," a famous sword from the ancient wars that had slain hundreds of goblins. To them, it was known simply as "Biter."

The polite fiction of the interrogation evaporated, replaced by a howl of pure, ancestral rage. "Murderers and elf-friends!" the Great Goblin screamed. "Slash them! Beat them! Bite them! Gnash them! Take them away to dark holes full of snakes, and never let them see the light again!" In his fury, he jumped from his seat and rushed at Thorin himself, his mouth open, ready to deliver the first savage blow.

6.0 A Miraculous Escape

Just as the Great Goblin lunged, a sudden and magical intervention threw the cavern into chaos. The same power that had saved Gandalf from capture now became the instrument of the company's salvation. All the lights went out. The great fire erupted into a tower of blue smoke and piercing white sparks that rained down upon the goblins, burning holes in them. The cavern filled with indescribable shrieks and howls as the goblins, blinded and panicked, fell into confusion, fighting amongst themselves.

In the midst of the pandemonium, a sword flashed with its own pale light. Bilbo saw it run the Great Goblin clean through. He fell dead, and his terrified soldiers fled shrieking into the darkness. It was Gandalf. He cut the dwarves’ chains with his sword, Glamdring, which the goblins called "Beater," and returned Orcrist to Thorin. After a quick roll-call confirmed all fourteen members were present, he urged them into the dark passages.

They fled, the dwarves taking turns carrying the slower Bilbo on their backs. Behind them, the sounds of an enraged goblin pursuit echoed closer and closer. At a sharp corner, Gandalf and Thorin fell to the rear to make a stand. As the goblins came scurrying around the turn, they were met by the cold, bright light of "Biter and Beater," the ancient Goblin-cleavers fulfilling their purpose once more in the deep places of the earth. The goblins at the front were killed instantly, while those behind fell back in shrieking confusion. This gave the company a precious head start, but the goblins did not give up; they merely changed their tactics.

7.0 The Unfortunate Separation

The frantic, noisy flight had ended, replaced by a new, more insidious danger. Halted by the elven blades, the goblins sent their quickest and quietest runners, who put on soft shoes and moved "as swift as weasels in the dark, and with hardly any more noise than bats." Unseen and unheard, they closed the distance as the company hurried on, Gandalf's wand casting only a faint light to guide them.

Dori, a decent fellow, was once again at the back of the line, carrying the weary Bilbo. In the near-total darkness, he was suddenly grabbed from behind. Dori shouted and fell. The sudden jolt sent the hobbit rolling from his shoulders into the pitch-blackness. As he tumbled, Bilbo's head struck hard rock, and he "remembered nothing more."

And so it was that the smallest member of the company was left utterly alone, lost in the black heart of the mountain—a turn of fate that would change not only his own destiny, but the course of a far greater history yet to be written.
A Detailed Summary of "Riddles in the Dark"

1. The Awakening in Darkness

When Bilbo opened his eyes, he wondered if he had; for it was just as dark as with them shut. Just imagine his fright! To be utterly alone in a darkness so profound that it was a physical presence, a silence so complete that it swallowed all sound. He could hear nothing, see nothing, and feel nothing except the cold, unforgiving stone of the floor. It was in this moment of absolute isolation, abandoned by dwarf and wizard alike, that Mr. Baggins was forced for the first time to rely not on the comforts of his hobbit-hole, but on the unplumbed resources of his own spirit.

Very slowly, he got to his feet and began to grope about on all fours, his head still swimming from his fall. He touched the wall of the tunnel, a slim guide in the endless black, but could find no sign of goblins, no sign of dwarves—nothing at all. As he crawled along, his hand suddenly met what felt like a tiny ring of cold metal lying on the floor. It was a turning point in his career, though he did not know it. He put the ring in his pocket almost without thinking; it seemed of no particular use at the moment. He did not go much further before giving himself up to complete miserableness, sitting on the cold floor and thinking of frying bacon and eggs in his own kitchen back home, a thought that only made him miserabler.

His despair was shattered, however, by a small, mundane discovery. In slapping at his pockets, he found his pipe and his tobacco pouch! But this small comfort was dashed by the crushing realization that he had no matches. Just as he felt wholly crushed, his searching hand came upon the hilt of the little sword he had taken from the trolls. He drew it out, and it shone with a pale, dim light before his eyes. "So it is an elvish blade, too," he thought. "And goblins are not very near, and yet not far enough." The sight of the blade, a thing of legend made in Gondolin for the goblin-wars, was a comfort, a glimmer of hope in the oppressive dark. He could not go back, nor sideways; the only thing to do was to go forward. And so, with his heart all of a patter and a pitter, Bilbo Baggins got up and trotted along, his little sword held out before him.

2. The Encounter at the Lake's Edge

On and on he went, and down and down. The tunnel seemed to have no end, a monotonous descent into the very roots of the mountain, the only sounds being the occasional whirr of a bat's wings. It seemed like all the way to tomorrow and over it to the days beyond. Then, suddenly and without warning, he trotted splash into icy cold water. It pulled him up sharp and short. Was it a pool, he wondered, or the edge of a subterranean stream, or the brink of a deep, dark lake? He could not swim, and his mind immediately filled with images of nasty, slimy things with big, bulging blind eyes wriggling in the unseen depths.

It was there, by the dark water, that he met old Gollum. From the shadows emerged a small, slimy creature, as dark as darkness itself, save for two big, round, pale eyes that shone like lamps in his thin face. He paddled a little boat with his large feet, never making a ripple, hunting blind fish with his long fingers. He liked meat, too, and thought goblin a tasty morsel, whom he would throttle from behind if they ever came near the water's edge alone. As Bilbo sat flummoxed and at his wit's end, Gollum came upon him and whispered and hissed, "Bless us and splash us, my precioussss! I guess it’s a choice feast; at least a tasty morsel it’d make us, gollum!" a sentiment punctuated by a horrible swallowing noise in his throat.

The hobbit jumped nearly out of his skin, thrusting his dagger before him. Gollum, for his part, was more curious than hungry. He did not quite like the look of the sword, a blade from Gondolin, and so he became quite polite. "Praps ye sits here and chats with it a bitsy, my preciousss," he hissed. "It likes riddles, praps it does, does it?" And so, in that damp, chilling darkness, a game was proposed, an ancient game with the highest of stakes. If Bilbo could ask a riddle Gollum could not answer, Gollum would show him the way out. But if Gollum won, if Bilbo failed to answer, then Gollum would eat him.

3. A Game of Wits for Life and Death

The riddle-game was an ancient and sacred tradition, and in the deep places of the world, even wicked creatures were afraid to cheat at it. For Bilbo, his very life hung in the balance; for Gollum, it was a chance for a meal and the first intellectual sport he had enjoyed in ages. With no other choice, Bilbo agreed, and the contest of wits began, a back-and-forth duel in the dark.

Gollum asked first:

What has roots as nobody sees, Is taller than trees, Up, up it goes, And yet never grows?

Bilbo, to his relief, found the answer simple enough, for such things were all around him. "Mountain, I suppose," he said.

It was Bilbo's turn:

Thirty white horses on a red hill, First they champ, Then they stamp, Then they stand still.

It was an old one, and Gollum knew it well, hissing, "Teeth! teeth! my preciousss; but we has only six!"

Gollum asked his second:

Voiceless it cries, Wingless flutters, Toothless bites, Mouthless mutters.

Bilbo had heard something like it before, and after a moment’s thought, he found the answer. "Wind, wind of course," he said, pleased enough to make one up on the spot.

Bilbo asked his third:

An eye in a blue face Saw an eye in a green face. "That eye is like to this eye" Said the first eye, "But in low place Not in high place."

Gollum hissed and spluttered, for it had been a long, long time since he had thought of such things. But then memories of a time before the darkness returned to him. "Sun on the daisies it means, it does," he said. The everyday riddles were tiring him, however, and making him hungry.

Gollum's next was more unpleasant:

It cannot be seen, cannot be felt, Cannot be heard, cannot be smelt. It lies behind stars and under hills, And empty holes it fills. It comes first and follows after, Ends life, kills laughter.

The answer was all around them, and Bilbo knew it at once. "Dark!" he said without hesitation.

To gain time, Bilbo offered a simple chestnut:

A box without hinges, key, or lid, Yet golden treasure inside is hid.

But it proved a nasty poser for Gollum, who hissed and whispered to himself but could not guess. At last, just as Bilbo grew impatient, Gollum remembered thieving from nests long ago. "Eggses!" he hissed. "Eggses it is!"

Gollum, flustered, asked his next:

Alive without breath, As cold as death; Never thirsty, ever drinking, All in mail never clinking.

This was a poser for poor Bilbo, who never had anything to do with water if he could help it. I imagine you know the answer, of course, since you are sitting comfortably at home and have not the danger of being eaten to disturb your thinking. As Gollum began to hiss with pleasure, "Is it nice, my preciousss? Is it juicy? Is it scrumptiously crunchable?", a fish jumped from the water in a fright and landed on Bilbo's toes. "Ugh! it is cold and clammy!" he said—and so he guessed. "Fish! fish!"

Bilbo, in a hurry, asked another:

No-legs lay on one-leg, two-legs sat near on three-legs, four-legs got some.

With fish already on his mind, Gollum soon gave the answer: "Fish on a little table, man at table sitting on a stool, the cat has the bones."

Then Gollum asked his final, horrible riddle:

This thing all things devours: Birds, beasts, trees, flowers; Gnaws iron, bites steel; Grinds hard stones to meal; Slays king, ruins town, And beats high mountain down.

Poor Bilbo was frightened, and that is bad for thinking. As Gollum began to get out of his boat, his eyes coming nearer, Bilbo wanted to shout for more time. But all that came out was a sudden squeal: "Time! Time!" By pure luck, that was the answer. The formal game was over, but a final, unexpected question was about to change everything.

4. The Unfair Question and the Lost Precious

Gollum was angry now, and very hungry. He sat down in the dark by Bilbo, whose wits were completely scattered by the nasty, wet, cold thing pawing and poking him. He simply could not think of a new riddle. "Ask us! ask us!" Gollum demanded. Bilbo pinched himself, slapped himself, and in his desperation, his hand felt about in his pocket. There he found the ring he had picked up and forgotten.

"What have I got in my pocket?" he said aloud. He was talking to himself, but Gollum took it for a riddle and was frightfully upset. "Not fair! not fair!" he hissed. "It isn't fair, my precious, is it, to ask us what it's got in its nassty little pocketses?" Bilbo, seeing his chance, stuck to the question, demanding three guesses.

Gollum guessed Handses!—wrong. He guessed Knife!—wrong again. After rocking and wriggling in a much worse state than before, he wasted his last guess, shrieking, "String, or nothing!" "Both wrong," cried Bilbo, relieved, and he jumped to his feet with his sword out. He knew the riddle-game was sacred, but he felt he could not trust this slimy creature to keep his promise. Gollum, however, did not attack. He asked to go and fetch "things to help us" from his island, and Bilbo, thinking it an excuse, let him go.

But Gollum did mean to come back. On his island, he kept a precious, beautiful thing: a golden ring of power that made its wearer invisible. "My birthday-present!" he whispered to himself. With it, he could creep up on the hobbit, and the nasty little sword would be useless. He slipped away into the dark, but a moment later, a bloodcurdling screech echoed across the water. "Where iss it? Where iss it?" Bilbo heard him cry. "Losst it is, my precious, lost, lost! Curse us and crush us, my precious is lost!"

A horrible suspicion began to dawn in Gollum's mind. His hissing grew louder and sharper as he demanded to know what was in Bilbo's pocketses. The pale light in his eyes became a green fire as he paddled wildly back to the shore. The game of wits had ended, and in its place was a deadly pursuit, for Gollum's suspicion had turned to a murderous rage that no sword could terrify.

5. Flight to the Back-Door

With Gollum's enraged hisses close behind, Bilbo turned and ran blindly back up the dark passage. The contest had become a desperate chase. He panted and stumbled, his left hand feeling the wall, his heart pounding. As he put that hand into his pocket, the ring felt very cold as it quietly slipped onto his groping forefinger. Terrified, he tried to run faster, but he struck his toes on a snag and fell flat. In a moment, Gollum was on him. But before Bilbo could do anything, Gollum passed right by, taking no notice, cursing and whispering as he ran.

What could it mean? Confused, Bilbo cautiously got up and followed. It was his only hope. He heard Gollum's curses ahead: "Curse the Baggins! It's gone! What has it got in its pocketses? Oh we guess, we guess, my precious. He's found it, yes he must have. My birthday-present." At last, Bilbo understood. The ring he had found was a magic ring—it made you invisible! He hurried after Gollum, whose chief fear now was that the goblins at the "back-door" would find the ring and become masters of invisibility themselves. He was, in his frantic search, leading Bilbo directly to an exit.

They came to a dead stop before a low opening. Gollum sat humped right in the passage, blocking the way, his eyes gleaming cold as he sniffed the air. Bilbo, knowing he must escape, was faced with a terrible choice. He must fight. He must kill the foul thing. But then, a sudden understanding, a pity mixed with horror, welled up in his heart: a glimpse of the creature's endless, miserable days without light or hope. He trembled. Then, in a flash, lifted by a new strength, he took a great leap in the dark, seven feet forward and three in the air, right over Gollum's head. Gollum's hands snapped on thin air as Bilbo landed on his sturdy feet and sped off, a final, despairing shriek of "Thief, thief, thief! Baggins! We hates it for ever!" echoing behind him.

Following the passage upwards, he saw a glimpse of pale, out-of-doors light. He ran, turning the last corner, and found himself at the goblin-gate, which stood ajar. But it was guarded. With yells of delight, the goblins rushed at him—but then he vanished. In his panic, he had struck his hands into his pockets and the ring had slipped on his finger. A horrible game of blind-man's-buff ensued as he dodged and scrambled for the door. He tried to squeeze through the crack but got stuck, his nice brass buttons wedged on the door-post. "There is a shadow by the door!" a goblin shouted. With a terrific squirm, buttons burst in all directions. He was through, leaping down the steps like a goat into the dazzling light, a free hobbit once more, as the bewildered goblins were left behind. He had escaped the darkness beneath the mountains, but he was no longer just the hobbit he had been.
A Detailed Summary of "Out of the Frying-Pan Into the Fire"

1.0 A Solitary Escape and a Moral Reckoning

Having slipped free from the subterranean clutches of the goblins, Bilbo Baggins finds himself not in a state of triumphant relief, but one of profound disorientation and loneliness. This moment, standing utterly alone on the far side of the Misty Mountains, marks a pivotal test of his character. Stripped of all comforts and companionship, he is forced to confront the harsh realities of the Wild and the unexpected weight of his own conscience, facing a decision that will define his burgeoning role within the company.

1.1 First Moments of Freedom

Bilbo’s initial taste of freedom is soured by the grim realization of his predicament. He has emerged from the goblin tunnels on the eastern side of the Misty Mountains, a landscape entirely unknown to him, and the sun is already sinking behind the peaks. His inventory of losses is stark and total, a testament to the harrowing ordeal he has just survived: “He had lost hood, cloak, food, pony, his buttons and his friends.” The absence of his companions weighs most heavily, leaving him isolated in an unforgiving wilderness with no clear path forward.

1.2 An Uncomfortable Duty

An intense internal conflict quickly consumes the hobbit. The desire for self-preservation—the familiar, comfortable Baggins instinct—battles with a newfound and deeply uncomfortable sense of duty. He contemplates the immense danger of returning to the “horrible, horrible, tunnels” to search for the dwarves and Gandalf. It is a terrifying prospect, one that goes against every fiber of his hobbit nature. Yet, the moral obligation to his friends, however troublesome he has found them, compels him to consider it. He has just resolved to turn back, a miserable but firm sense of duty winning out, when a faint sound cuts through the silence—not the harsh tongue of goblins, but voices that carry an unexpected promise of reunion.

2.0 An Unexpected Reunion and a Newfound Respect

The reunion between Bilbo and the company proves to be a fundamental turning point in their journey. This event single-handedly transforms the dwarves' perception of the hobbit. No longer is he merely the inconvenient baggage they were saddled with; through his seemingly impossible escape and miraculous reappearance, he earns their genuine admiration, cementing his place as a valued and respected member of the expedition.

2.1 The Debate

Creeping forward, Bilbo discovers Gandalf and the dwarves huddled in a dell, debating what to do next. The dwarves are grumbling, questioning why Bilbo was ever brought along and complaining about the prospect of returning to the tunnels to find him. Gandalf, however, defends the hobbit fiercely. To their complaints, the wizard retorts angrily, "I brought him, and I don’t bring things that are of no use. Either you help me to look for him, or I go and leave you here to get out of the mess as best you can yourselves." He insists that they will thank him for bringing Bilbo before their quest is over.

2.2 The Burglar’s Reappearance

Just as the argument reaches its peak, Bilbo decides to give them a surprise. With his magic ring still on his finger, he is completely invisible. "And here’s the burglar!" said Bilbo, stepping down into the middle of them and slipping off the ring. The company jumps in shock, which quickly turns to shouts of surprise and delight. Gandalf is as astonished as any of them, while Balin, who was on lookout duty, is utterly puzzled as to how the hobbit crept past him unseen. Cleverly, Bilbo omits any mention of the magic ring, attributing his success merely to having "just crept along, you know—very carefully and quietly."

2.3 A Reputation Transformed

This masterful reappearance has an immediate and profound impact on Bilbo’s standing. From that moment, the dwarves' perception of him was transformed; their skepticism melted away, replaced by a deep and genuine respect. Balin, the most puzzled of all, removes his hood in a gesture of deference. If the dwarves "had still doubted that he was really a first-class burglar, in spite of Gandalf’s words, they doubted no longer." They listen with rapt attention to the tale of his adventures, particularly his riddle-competition with Gollum, and their admiration grows.

The joy of their reunion is short-lived, however, as Gandalf quickly calls them to their senses, reminding them of the pressing dangers that still surround them.

3.0 A Wizard’s Counsel and a Perilous Journey Onward

With the company reunited, Gandalf provides a sobering assessment of their situation. The wizard's strategic knowledge of the mountains and their inhabitants reveals the true extent of their peril. His counsel underscores the grim reality of their location and confirms the absolute necessity of their immediate flight, propelling them onward into the next phase of their perilous journey.

3.1 A Grim Reality

Gandalf explains his own narrow escape, revealing he was aware of the goblins’ "back-door" on the eastern side of the mountains. He confirms Bilbo’s fears about their location, stating they are "too far to the North, and have some awkward country ahead. And we are still pretty high up." Time is of the essence, as the goblins will soon be out in the hundreds hunting them once night falls. They must put many miles between themselves and the tunnels before dusk.

3.2 Hunger and Fatigue

The company was in a dire state, their strength utterly spent. Exhausted and having lost all their provisions and ponies in the goblin tunnels, they were weak with hunger. Their plight is perfectly captured by Bilbo, who, upon realizing there is nothing to eat, lets out a groan that he is "dreadfully hungry." With no other choice, Gandalf urges them to "just tighten our belts and trudge on," lest they be made into the goblins' supper.

3.3 Down the Stone-Slide

Their subsequent journey is a chaotic descent down a wide, steep slope of fallen stones, the remnants of a landslide. The unstable footing quickly gives way, and the entire slope begins to move. They find themselves sliding away in a "fearful confusion of slipping, rattling, cracking slabs and stones." Their uncontrolled descent is only halted by a pine wood at the bottom of the slope. Some of the company manage to grab hold of tree trunks, while others, like Bilbo, shelter behind them from the onslaught of rocks.

The immediate danger of the slide is over, but the forest into which they have stumbled offers little comfort. As they limp southward, the gloom deepens, the silence becomes profound, and an unsettling atmosphere settles over them, portending the new threat that lies just ahead.

4.0 The Howling Glade and the Siege of the Wargs

The company's flight from the generalized danger of the mountains soon gives way to a specific, immediate, and terrifying threat. After a weary trek through the darkening pine forest, they emerge into what appears to be a ring of trees. This glade, however, is no refuge. It is a trap—a sacred meeting place for the evil Wargs, one of the most fearsome predators in the Wild.

4.1 The Howling Begins

In the unsettling quiet of the clearing, a long, shuddering howl echoes from the hills below. It is answered by another, far to the right, and then another, much nearer on the left. The howls draw closer from all sides until the company realizes with a jolt of terror that they are surrounded. Bilbo recognizes the sound from tales and cries out in despair, "Escaping goblins to be caught by wolves!" These are no ordinary wolves but the evil Wargs. As the source material warns, "Even magic rings are not much use against wolves." The howls close in, and soon hundreds of eyes are glaring at them from the darkness.

4.2 A Frantic Scramble

"Up the trees quick!" cries Gandalf, and the entire company scrambles for safety in the branches of the pines surrounding the glade. The scene is both desperate and comical, with the dwarves perched high in the trees, their "beards dangling down, like old gentlemen gone cracked." Dori, who is in the lowest and easiest tree, has to climb down and allow the hobbit to stand on his back to get high enough to climb. He swings himself up just in time, as a wolf snaps at his cloak, narrowly missing him.

4.3 The Wargs' Council

The glade, it becomes clear, is a meeting place for the wolves. Hundreds and hundreds of Wargs gather in a great circle, with a large grey wolf acting as their chief in the center. Gandalf, who can understand the "dreadful language of the Wargs," listens to their council. He discovers that they are waiting to meet a party of goblins. Together, they have planned a great raid for that very night on the nearby villages of men, intending to kill and devour the inhabitants. The dwarves, they believe, are spies for the woodmen, and they have no intention of letting them escape.

Refusing to be a passive victim awaiting his doom, Gandalf resolves not to let the Wargs have it all their own way, and prepares to launch a magical assault from his perch in the tall pine.

5.0 Fire and Fury: Gandalf's Counter-Attack

With the company trapped and surrounded, Gandalf once again assumes his role as their protector. In this seemingly hopeless situation, the wizard’s command of magic becomes their only strategic advantage. His fiery assault transforms the tense siege into a scene of blazing chaos, but it also draws the attention of an even more dangerous foe.

5.1 The Wizard's Fire

From his high perch, Gandalf gathers huge pine-cones and, using his magic, sets them ablaze with blue, red, and green fire. He hurls these flaming projectiles whizzing down into the circle of Wargs below. The effect is immediate and devastating. Wolves are terrified of fire, and this is an "uncanny fire" that sticks and burns in their shaggy coats. The glade erupts in commotion as the wolves yelp in pain and terror, rolling frantically on the ground to extinguish the sparks amid the smell of burning fur.

5.2 A Forest Ablaze

The counter-attack has an unintended and dangerous consequence. The wolves that are set alight flee in panic, running howling into the woods. In their flight, they set the dry summer forest ablaze in several places, and soon flames are leaping up all around the clearing.

5.3 The Goblins Arrive

Just then, the goblins arrive for their meeting. Far from being afraid of the fire, they are cruelly amused by the dwarves' predicament and quickly devise a plan. They gather brushwood and fern, and they "fed the flames" nearest to the trees where the company is trapped. They carefully manage the blaze, preventing it from spreading outward but allowing it to close in, creating a "ring of smoke and flame" that slowly tightens around the dwarves.

5.4 A Cruel Song

As the flames draw nearer, the goblins dance around the ring of fire, singing a horrible, taunting song to their trapped victims:

Fifteen birds in five fir-trees, their feathers were fanned in a fiery breeze! But, funny little birds, they had no wings! O what shall we do with the funny little things?

The song continues, gleefully describing how they will roast, boil, and eat the dwarves as the fire licks higher up the trunks of the trees.

The situation becomes utterly desperate. The bark of Gandalf's tree catches fire, and the lower branches begin to crack. Seeing no other escape, the wizard climbs to the very top, preparing to make a final, suicidal leap down among the goblins' spears.

6.0 Salvation from the Sky

Just as all hope seems lost—with flames licking the highest branches and Gandalf preparing for a final leap—salvation arrives from the moonlit peaks. The majestic appearance of the great eagles serves as a classic deus ex machina, a powerful, external force that intervenes to resolve an otherwise certain catastrophe, plucking the adventurers from the very jaws of death.

6.1 The Lord of the Eagles

High on a lonely pinnacle, the Lord of the Eagles has his curiosity piqued by the commotion in the forest below. His people hold an ancient enmity for goblins, and seeing the glint of their spears and the flashes of fire, he summons many of his followers and soars down from the mountains to investigate.

6.2 The Rescue

The arrival of the eagles is swift and decisive, coming down "like huge black shadows." The Lord of the Eagles seizes Gandalf in his talons first, just as the wizard is about to leap. The rest of the eagles then fall upon the goblins and Wargs, smiting them to the ground with the "dark rush of their beating wings" and tearing at their faces with their talons, scattering the terrified enemy in all directions.

6.3 A Precarious Escape

Other eagles fly to the treetops to rescue the dwarves, who are scrambling as high as they can to escape the flames. Poor Bilbo is nearly left behind again but just manages to "catch hold of Dori’s legs" as Dori is carried away last of all. He is borne up into the night sky, swinging precariously in the air with his arms nearly breaking.

6.4 A Terrifying Flight

For Bilbo, who has a fear of heights, the flight is a terrifying ordeal. He looks down between his dangling toes at the dark lands opening wide beneath him and feels his head swim. The experience is so disorienting and strange that it provokes one of his most memorable observations: "Now I know what a piece of bacon feels like when it is suddenly picked out of the pan on a fork and put back on the shelf!"

The flight ends just before his arms give way, as the eagles deposit the entire company safely upon a wide shelf of rock high on the mountainside—their eyrie, a temporary sanctuary from the night's horrors.

7.0 Respite on the Eyrie

The eagles' eyrie represents a crucial interlude of safety and recovery. This high, inaccessible shelf of rock offers the company a brief but vital moment of respite, allowing them to tend to their wounds, satisfy their hunger, and plan the next stage of their quest. This sanctuary effectively marks the end of their perilous and harrowing passage through the Misty Mountains.

7.1 Prisoners Rescued

Upon arriving, Bilbo learns that he and the dwarves are not captives. The messenger eagle had referred to them as 'prisoners' only in the sense that they were "prisoners rescued from the goblins." Gandalf and the Lord of the Eagles are on friendly terms; the wizard had once rendered a great service to the eagles by healing their lord from an "arrow-wound." The rescue is thus, in part, a repayment of this old debt.

7.2 Negotiations for Transport

Gandalf immediately begins discussing plans with the Great Eagle to transport the company further along on their journey. The Lord of the Eagles agrees to help but refuses to take them anywhere too close to where men live. "They would shoot at us with their great bows of yew," he explains, fearing they would be mistaken for hunting sheep. They will carry the company far away from the mountains but must set them down before they reach the lowlands.

7.3 A Welcome Feast

Seeing their famished state, the Lord of the Eagles declares that their hunger "can perhaps be mended." His followers bring dry boughs for a fire and provide the company with a much-needed feast of "rabbits, hares, and a small sheep." The dwarves manage the preparations, and soon the smell of roasting meat fills the air, bringing immense comfort to the weary and starving travelers.

Having finally eaten his fill, Bilbo curls up on the hard rock and sleeps more soundly than he ever did in his own feather bed. His mind, however, wanders back to his hobbit-hole, and "all night he dreamed of his own house...looking for something that he could not find nor remember what it looked like." This dream illustrates his deep-seated longing for the simple comforts of home, a stark contrast to the great and terrible adventures that still lie ahead.
A Detailed Summary of "Queer Lodgings"

1. The Eagles' Flight and Arrival at the Carrock

Our tale resumes in the cool light of morning, immediately following the company's harrowing escape from the goblins and their Warg allies. The intervention of the great eagles was a rescue of the most pivotal kind, a sudden and breathtaking flight that snatched the dwarves and their hobbit from the very jaws of peril. This aerial journey was more than a mere escape; it was a transportation from the familiar dangers of the mountains to the threshold of a new and unknown land. For Bilbo Baggins, it marked another step in his confrontation with a world far larger and more dangerous than he had ever imagined, a trial by terror and height that he was forced to endure.

Bilbo awoke to find himself perched high upon a mountain's side. The breakfast that followed was a stark affair of cold mutton and rabbit, a grim reminder of the night's terrors. Soon after, the company took to the air once more, each borne upon the back of a mighty eagle. As you can imagine, Bilbo was at first overcome with fear, clutching tightly to his feathered transport until the great bird spoke reassuringly to him. “Don’t pinch!” it said. “You need not be frightened like a rabbit, even if you look rather like one.” Peeping through his fingers, the hobbit saw the world spread far below, the misty lands falling away behind them as they soared eastward. Their flight concluded upon a great rock, almost a hill of stone, that stood alone in the path of a river. This was the Carrock. Here, upon a flat space at its summit, the eagles set them down and bid them farewell. The company made their way down a well-worn path of many steps that led to a stony ford, and near the river's edge, they gathered in a small, wholesome cave to discuss what was to be done.

It was here that Gandalf announced his intention to leave them, and upon hearing these words, the dwarves groaned in distress while poor Bilbo simply wept.

2. Gandalf's Plan and the Nature of Beorn

Gandalf's declaration marked a critical turning point for the company, for with it came a profound shift in their fortunes and their psychology. They had come to rely upon the wizard as their guide and protector, a seemingly endless source of aid in moments of direst need. His departure signalled the end of their guided adventure and the true beginning of a perilous quest, forcing a shift from reliance on magic to a dependence on their own mettle. They were to be cast upon the hospitality of a mysterious and dangerous stranger, foreshadowing the trials that would soon demand Bilbo's evolution from passenger to protagonist.

The wizard explained that his part of the adventure was always to see them safely over the mountains, and this, by good management and good luck, he had now done. He spoke of other pressing business far away that he must attend to, but not before seeing them out of their current plight. They had no food, no baggage, and no ponies, and so Gandalf proposed they seek aid from a "Somebody" he knew—the very individual who had carved the steps upon the Carrock. The dwarves pleaded with him, offering dragon-gold and jewels to persuade him to stay, but the wizard remained firm, refusing to change his mind.

This mysterious host, Gandalf revealed, was a great person named Beorn. He was, the wizard explained, a "skin-changer," a being who could take the form of either a huge black bear or a great, strong, black-haired man with immense arms and a mighty beard. His origins were a matter of some debate; some said he was a bear descended from the great and ancient bears of the mountains, while others believed he was a man descended from the first men who lived before Smaug and the goblins arrived in those lands. He lived a self-sufficient life within an oak-wood, keeping cattle and horses which were nearly as marvellous as himself and could understand his speech. He hunted no wild animals, but lived mostly on cream and honey from the hives of his great, fierce bees. He was, Gandalf warned, under no enchantment but his own and could be appalling when angered, which he was wont to do easily. He was a great enemy of goblins, but Gandalf noted that he had a good cousin in the wizard Radagast.

Hearing this description, the company was filled with apprehension, and they proceeded towards Beorn's home with the utmost caution.

3. A Cunning Introduction

An introduction to a character as formidable and easily angered as Beorn required a careful and cunning strategy, a piece of theatre orchestrated by Gandalf with masterful insight. To arrive upon his doorstep with a company of fourteen unannounced guests—especially dwarves, for whom Beorn was "not over fond"—would have been an act of supreme folly, almost certainly doomed to failure. The wizard’s plan was therefore one of subtle stagecraft, designed to manipulate Beorn's core traits—his deep-seated hatred of goblins, his grudging curiosity, and his fierce territoriality—to secure his hospitality before his temper could get the better of him.

Their journey brought them to the edge of Beorn's bee-pastures, where giant bees droned over great patches of clover, and eventually to a high thorn-hedge with a broad wooden gate. Here, Gandalf gave his precise instructions: he and Bilbo would go on ahead, and the dwarves were to follow in pairs at intervals of five minutes. The portly Bombur, counting for two, was to come last and alone. The wizard and the hobbit pushed through the gate and soon came upon Beorn himself, a huge man leaning on a great axe. He was gruff and questioning, but his manner softened slightly at the mention of Gandalf's good cousin, Radagast. Then, sitting upon the veranda of Beorn's long wooden house, Gandalf began the tale of their adventures.

Just as he was describing their flight from the goblins, he gave a long, shrill whistle. As if by accident, Thorin and Dori appeared and stood bowing low. Beorn growled, but his interest was caught. The story went on, cleverly interrupted every five minutes by the arrival of another pair of dwarves. First came Nori and Ori; then Balin and Dwalin, whose comical and excessively polite bowing made Beorn chuckle; followed by Fili and Kili; then Oin and Gloin; after them, Bifur and Bofur; and at the very last, puffing and red-faced, came Bombur. With each new arrival, Beorn grumbled and frowned, yet he could not bring himself to turn them away, for he had grown ever more engrossed in Gandalf's tale of their clash with the Great Goblin and their desperate escape.

In this masterful way, through a story strategically told and perfectly timed, Gandalf secured for the entire company an invitation to supper and a promise of safe lodging for the night.

4. Hospitality and a Night of Mystery

The atmosphere within Beorn's hall was one of a strange and rustic magic, a place of comforting warmth and safety. This form of hospitality, however, was "queer"—a sanctuary provided by a being who was himself half-wild, a protector who was as much a part of the wilderness as the dangers from which he sheltered them. For the weary travellers, this respite was a moment of profound significance, a sheltered harbour between the storm-wracked peaks of the Misty Mountains and the looming, oppressive darkness of Mirkwood forest that lay just ahead.

Supper was a marvellous affair. Beorn clapped his hands and in trotted his wonderful animals to serve them. Four beautiful white ponies and several large, long-bodied grey dogs carried torches in their mouths, while snow-white sheep, led by a great coal-black ram, bore trays laden with bowls, platters, and wooden spoons. As they ate and drank from bowls of mead, Beorn told them tales of the wild lands, speaking gravely of the perils of the terrible forest of Mirkwood.

After the meal, the dwarves began to sing. But as their songs filled the hall, Beorn seemed to grow drowsy and soon departed, leaving them with Gandalf's stark and final warning: they must not, on their peril, stray outside the hall until the sun was up. The night that followed was an uneasy one for Bilbo. He lay huddled in his bed and heard, from beyond the door, a growling sound and the noise of some great animal scuffling. He wondered if it was Beorn in his enchanted shape and, fearing the worst, hid his head under the blankets until he at last fell asleep.

When the company awoke the next morning, it was to find the hall empty, for Beorn himself was gone.

5. Beorn's Return and Solemn Counsel

The mystery of the previous night, and of their host's strange disappearance, was soon resolved in a most conclusive fashion. Beorn, it turned out, was not one to take a tale at face value. His independent verification of the company's story was a pivotal moment, transforming him from a suspicious, if intrigued, host into a true and committed ally, for he had sought out and found his own proof of their extraordinary adventure.

Gandalf, rising early, went out and discovered a multitude of bear-tracks leading away from the house, evidence of a "regular bears' meeting." One set of tracks, however, he followed to the Carrock and saw that they vanished into the river. From this, he surmised that Beorn himself, in his great bear-form, had travelled back towards the mountains to confirm their tale. It was not until the evening of the next day that Beorn returned, now in a splendidly good humour. He told them he had caught a Warg and a goblin in the woods and forced from them the news of the patrols that were still hunting for the dwarves. Having seen the truth of their tale for himself—and holding a newfound respect for them for having killed the Great Goblin—he now offered his full help. As grim evidence of his errand, a goblin's head was stuck outside his gate, and a warg-skin was nailed to a nearby tree.

Beorn's aid was both generous and practical, but it came with counsel of the most solemn and urgent nature. He provided them with the following:

* Provisions: Ponies for each of the dwarves and a horse for Gandalf. For their packs, he gave them nuts, flour, sealed jars of dried fruits, red earthenware pots of honey, and special twice-baked cakes that would keep for a long time. He also gave them skins for carrying water, and bows and arrows.
* Warnings: He delivered three critical pieces of advice for their passage through Mirkwood. First, that the wild things in the forest were "dark, queer, and savage." Second, that they must neither drink from nor bathe in the black enchanted stream that crossed the path, for it carried a great drowsiness and forgetfulness. And third, the most important warning of all: for any reason, they MUST NOT stray from the path.

Their spirits, which had soared with Beorn's friendship, sank once more upon hearing these grave words as they began their final preparations.

6. The Edge of Mirkwood and Gandalf's Farewell

The journey to the forest's edge represented the final stage of their travels under Gandalf's watchful eye, and his departure marked the true beginning of the quest in earnest. It was a moment of profound significance, for the company, and especially Bilbo, would now have to rely entirely upon their own courage, their own wits, and their own resources. The safety net was gone; from this point forward, the hobbit's transformation from dependent to leader would become a matter of survival for them all.

Following Beorn's advice, they rode north, and on the third night, as dusk fell, Bilbo was sure he saw the shadowy form of a great bear prowling along in the same direction, but Gandalf simply bid him to "Take no notice!" By the afternoon of the fourth day, they reached the eaves of Mirkwood. The forest loomed before them like a black and frowning wall, its outer trees huge and gnarled, with dark, twisted branches and trailing ivy. Here, Gandalf instructed them to send back the ponies and horse, explaining that Beorn loved his animals as his children and had, in his bear-form, kept a secret watch over them to ensure their safety.

Then came the wizard's final farewell. He reiterated his reasons for leaving, speaking again of pressing business in the south, but he left them with words of encouragement, placing his faith especially in Mr. Baggins. The hobbit, filled with despair, made a final plea, asking if there was no way around the terrible wood. Gandalf explained that there was not; to the north lay the goblin-infested slopes of the Grey Mountains, and to the south lay the dreadful land of the Necromancer, a place they must avoid at all costs.

Before he galloped away, he turned and gave them one last, echoing shout, words that would follow them into the shadows: “Good-bye! Be good, take care of yourselves—and DON’T LEAVE THE PATH!” Then he was gone, and the thirteen companions shouldered their heavy packs and plunged into the forest.
A Detailed Summary of 'Flies and Spiders'

Introduction: The Oppressive Gloom of Mirkwood

Deep within the perilous forest of Mirkwood, the company of Thorin Oakenshield finds itself on the brink of despair. Hope, once a flickering flame, is now dwindling to a mere ember in the face of an endless, suffocating darkness. Having journeyed far beyond the lands they knew, the dwarves and their hobbit companion are now beset by an oppressive gloom that weighs as heavily on their spirits as the thick, motionless air of the forest weighs on their lungs. The path, their only guide, winds onward through an unchanging landscape of ancient, gnarled trees, and with each weary step, the memory of sunlight and open sky fades, replaced by a growing sense of dread and exhaustion that marks the beginning of their most trying ordeal.

1. The Enchanted Stream and the Fateful Slumber

This passage marks a critical turning point, where the company's resolve finally shatters under the combined assault of physical hardship and unseen magic. Starvation and thirst had already worn them to their limits, but it is the encounter with the enchanted stream—a tangible manifestation of Beorn's stark warning—that precipitates their first catastrophic error. Their desperation, honed by days of suffering, leads them to take a series of fateful actions that will ultimately leave them vulnerable, defenceless, and hopelessly lost.

Narration of Hardships

The journey through Mirkwood had become a waking nightmare. They travelled through a perpetual twilight, breathing stuffy, motionless air under a canopy so dense that it choked the sky. The forest floor was piled with the dead leaves of countless autumns, and every surface was draped in thick, dark cobwebs. Their nights were even worse, filled with queer scuffling noises and the terrifying appearance of disembodied eyes—yellow, red, or pale and bulbous—that would gleam at them from the oppressive blackness. Their attempts to light watch-fires proved disastrous; the flames attracted swarms of giant, dark-grey moths and great black bats, forcing them to endure the cold and the uncanny darkness without comfort.

Their provisions dwindled alarmingly. In their hunger, they wasted precious arrows attempting to hunt the black squirrels that whisked across their path, only to discover that the meat was horrible to taste. Thirst became a constant torment, as they had seen neither spring nor stream since entering the wood.

The Black Water

One day, their path was abruptly blocked by a running stream. Its water was black and swift, flowing silently across their way. Beorn had warned them of this enchanted river, cautioning them never to drink from it or touch its waters. Their immediate concern, however, was how to cross. On the far bank, Bilbo, whose eyes were sharpest, spotted a small boat. Fili, the youngest and most keen-sighted of the dwarves, was chosen for the task of retrieving it. Armed with a long rope and an iron hook, he cast across the stream. After one failed attempt that fell short, his second throw landed true, and the hook caught on the boat.

Bombur's Fall

With great effort, Fili, Kili, Oin, and Gloin hauled on the rope, pulling the boat across the stream. As they secured it, a great deer suddenly burst from the gloom, charging into the dwarves and scattering them before it gathered itself and leaped powerfully over the water. Thorin, the only one to have kept his wits, had his bow ready. He loosed a swift arrow, and the great hart stumbled as it landed on the far bank, its faltering hooves falling silent in the shadows.

In the ensuing chaos, however, disaster struck. Bombur, who had only one foot on the bank, was knocked over by the deer. He toppled backward into the black water, and though he was quickly pulled out with a rope, he was already in a deep, magical sleep. He became an immense, dead weight—a heavy burden that the already exhausted company was now forced to carry. This ill-fated decision not only plunged Bombur into an enchanted slumber but also wasted one of their last precious arrows, a loss that would soon prove critical and set the stage for even greater despair.

2. The Lure of the Phantom Feast

With their spirits at their lowest and starvation gnawing at them, the company becomes psychologically vulnerable. Bombur's beautiful dreams of a great woodland feast, recounted upon his brief waking, amplify their own desperate hunger. This intense longing makes them susceptible to the powerful elven magic that flickers at the edge of their perception, a deceptive lure that will ultimately draw them from the safety of the path and into ruin.

Bilbo's Ascent

In a desperate attempt to gauge their progress, the dwarves decided to send Bilbo up the tallest tree they could find. The hobbit, though unpractised in climbing, scrambled his way to the top. After a dreadful struggle, he poked his head above the endless canopy of leaves and was greeted by the brilliant sun, a refreshing breeze, and the sight of hundreds of velvety black butterflies fluttering about. He gazed in every direction but could see no end to the forest, only a vast sea of dark green leaves. Unbeknownst to him, his tree stood in a wide valley, so the surrounding forest swelled up like the rim of a great bowl, obscuring the true edge which was not far off. He climbed down with a despairing report, extinguishing the company’s last flicker of hope.

Desperation and Hallucination

Just as morale collapsed, Bombur awoke from his slumber. Weak and weeping, he tearfully described his dream of a great feast held by a woodland king, filling the minds of the starving dwarves with visions of endless food and drink. The tale only heightened their misery.

It was in this state of profound despair that they first saw the lights. A twinkle appeared in the woods to the left of their path, and it was soon joined by others. The memory of Bombur’s dream and the gnawing in their bellies was too much to resist. A fierce debate erupted, but hunger won out. Ignoring the dire warnings of both Gandalf and Beorn, they made the fateful decision to leave the path and pursue the phantom feast.

Threefold Deception

Their pursuit led to a series of magical deceptions, each one leaving them more lost and confused than the last.

* First Attempt: They peered into a clearing to see many elvish-looking folk feasting merrily. The smell of roast meats was so powerful that the dwarves rushed forward to beg for food. The moment the first dwarf set foot in the clearing, all the lights went out as if by magic, and the fire vanished, leaving them in absolute darkness.
* Second Attempt: After blundering about and finally regrouping, they saw the lights appear again. This time, they pushed Bilbo forward to parley. But as he stumbled into the torchlight, the feast vanished once more, and he was temporarily separated from his friends.
* Third Attempt: Roused later by the sound of harps and singing, they discovered a third, even more magnificent feast. At its head sat a woodland king with a crown of leaves. Unable to restrain himself, Thorin stepped boldly into the circle. Instantly, all light was extinguished, the fires exploded into black smoke, and in the ensuing chaos, the entire company was scattered, leaving Bilbo utterly alone. He sat with his back to a tree, his thoughts drifting to his comfortable hobbit-hole and its well-stocked pantries, before a faint touch—a strong, sticky thread against his hand—jolted him back to the terrifying reality of the forest.

3. A Hobbit's Courage and a Sword's New Name

This solitary confrontation marks the single most important turning point for Bilbo Baggins. Left alone in the pitch-dark, facing a monstrous foe without aid, he is forced to rely on his own wits and nerve. The ensuing struggle transforms him from a frightened, often reluctant companion into a resourceful and courageous hero, an evolution crystallized in the naming of his blade.

The First Kill

As Bilbo sat lost in thought, a great spider seized him. It moved with sickening speed, binding his legs in its thick, sticky threads while attempting to poison him with its sting to keep him still. In this moment of desperate struggle, with his life hanging by a thread, Bilbo remembered his sword. He drew the small elvish dagger and fought back with a fury he did not know he possessed. The spider, unused to prey that fought back with such a sharp sting, recoiled, giving Bilbo just enough time to cut his legs free. He then pressed his attack, stabbing the creature directly in its eyes. The spider went mad, thrashing its legs in horrible jerks, until a final stroke from Bilbo ended its life.

The Naming of Sting

The victory had a profound effect on Bilbo. Wiping the black stain from his blade, he felt a fundamental shift within himself. The act of killing the giant spider, all by himself in the dark, "made a great difference to Mr. Baggins. He felt a different person, and much fiercer and bolder." To commemorate this transformation and honor the weapon that saved him, he bestowed upon it a name, declaring aloud to the silent forest: "I will give you a name... and I shall call you Sting." With this act, a new resolve was born, and Bilbo’s internal transformation immediately gave way to a clear-eyed purpose: he had to find his lost friends.

4. Rescue from the Spiders

The spiders of Mirkwood were not solitary hunters but a vast and loathsome colony, presenting a formidable obstacle for any who strayed from the path. Having discovered his own courage, Bilbo now faced a test of his cleverness and leadership. His daring plan to rescue the dwarves would require every ounce of his new-found bravery, pushing him to take risks he would never have imagined only days before.

Discovery and Distraction

Slipping on his ring, Bilbo moved stealthily through the woods, searching for his companions. He soon came upon a place of utter blackness, a hollow draped in a nightmarish tangle of webs. Here, he discovered the spiders' colony and saw a horrifying sight: the dwarves, each wrapped in a thick web-bundle, hung from a high branch like grotesque fruit. He crept closer and overheard the spiders' creaking, hissing voices discussing their captives:

"It was a sharp struggle, but worth it," said one. "What nasty thick skins they have to be sure, but I’ll wager there is good juice inside."

"Aye, they’ll make fine eating, when they’ve hung a bit," said another.

Realizing he had to act, Bilbo devised a plan of distraction. He began throwing stones, knocking spiders from their perches and enraging the colony. To draw them away from their prisoners, he composed and sang two taunting songs, his voice echoing through the glade.

His first song was:

Old fat spider spinning in a tree! Old fat spider can’t see me! Attercop! Attercop! Won’t you stop, Stop your spinning and look for me?

And his second:

Lazy Lob and crazy Cob are weaving webs to wind me. I am far more sweet than other meat, but still they cannot find me!

The Desperate Battle

As the infuriated spiders chased after the unseen voice, Bilbo stole back to the rescue. He scrambled up a hanging thread and cut the dwarves free one by one, while an old slow wicked fat-bodied spider who had remained behind to guard the prisoners stood watch. Soon, the other spiders returned, and a desperate battle erupted. The dwarves, sick from poison and weak from hunger, fought for their lives with knives, sticks, and stones against hundreds of enraged creatures.

Seeing they were on the verge of being overwhelmed, Bilbo made a critical decision. He revealed the secret of his magic ring to the dwarves, instructing them to flee while he created a final, daring diversion. He vanished from their sight and charged the spiders, darting in and out, his blade Sting flashing as he protected their retreat.

Aftermath and a Missing Leader

The company finally escaped to a clearing where the faint remnants of elven magic still lingered, a power the spiders seemed to fear. There, they took stock of their situation. The dwarves looked upon Bilbo with a new and profound respect. They now saw him not as a burden, but as a companion of great wits, incredible luck, and the owner of a most useful magic ring. Their relief, however, was short-lived. As they counted themselves, a shocking realization dawned upon them: Thorin, their leader, was not among them. The successful rescue from one peril immediately gave way to another, and as the company stood leaderless and lost, their attention turned from their own survival to the unknown and likely grim fate of Thorin Oakenshield.

5. The Prisoner of the Elvenking

To understand Thorin's fate, the chronicle must turn back to the moment the third elven-feast vanished. The Wood-elves of Mirkwood were not like the High Elves of the West; they were less wise, more dangerous, and deeply distrustful of strangers. This inherent wariness, compounded by an ancient and bitter grudge against dwarves over treasure, meant that Thorin was judged a foe the moment he was discovered. It was this deep-seated suspicion, rather than any overt act of aggression, that sealed his fate and set the stage for the company's next great challenge.

Thorin's Capture

When Thorin stepped into the third and most magnificent of the elven-feasts, he did not fall into a mere illusion. The magic of the Wood-elves was strong, and he was instantly enchanted, falling like a stone. While his companions were scattered into the darkness, the elves bound him and carried him away to the great cave of their king.

The Halls of the King

The Elvenking’s palace was a vast cavern winding deep into a wooded hillside. Though a subterranean dwelling, it was lighter and more wholesome than any goblin-hold. Yet it was also a fortress and, for those who earned the king’s displeasure, a prison. The king’s suspicion of Thorin was fueled by an old and bitter history between their peoples. The dwarves accused the Elvenking of refusing to pay them for shaping his raw gold and silver, while the elves accused the dwarves of theft. This ancient quarrel, combined with the Elvenking's great love of treasure, ensured Thorin would receive no warm welcome.

The Interrogation

Thorin was brought before the Elvenking, who sat on a chair of carven wood with a staff of oak in his hand. The king looked at him sternly and demanded to know why the dwarves were troubling his people and what business had brought them into his realm. Knowing the history between their kinds, and determined that no word of his quest for the treasure of Smaug should be revealed, Thorin refused to answer, stating only that he and his folk were starving. His stubborn silence angered the king, who commanded:

“Take him away and keep him safe, until he feels inclined to tell the truth, even if he waits a hundred years.”

Thorin was taken to one of the inmost dungeons and locked behind a strong wooden door. He was given food and drink, for the Wood-elves were not cruel like goblins. But he remained a prisoner, cut off from his friends and unable to lead them, leaving the future of the quest entirely in the hands of a small, frightened, but increasingly resourceful hobbit.
The Hobbit's Undertaking: From Bag End to the Edge of the Wild

1.0 An Unexpected Party and a Quest Revealed

In a comfortable hole in the ground there lived a hobbit named Baggins, a very respectable and well-to-do hobbit, for whom the very notion of an adventure was a nasty, disturbing, uncomfortable thing that made one late for dinner. Yet, within this hobbit, Bilbo by name, lay a dormant seed of his mother’s blood, the famous Belladonna Took. For the Tooks were not quite like other hobbits; there was something a bit queer in their make-up, something that only waited for a chance to come out. That chance arrived one fine morning in the form of a wandering wizard, Gandalf, who, after a confounding conversation about the many uses of “Good morning,” scratched a queer sign upon Mr. Baggins’s beautiful green door and departed, leaving the hobbit more than a little flustered.

The consequences of this mark were not long in coming. The very next day, what began as tea with Gandalf turned into a bewildering invasion. One by one, and then in droves, thirteen dwarves descended upon Bag End, hanging their coloured hoods upon the pegs and calling for ale and seed-cakes and pork-pie until Bilbo’s larder was in peril and his wits were altogether bewuthered. They were a proud and haughty company, led by the great Thorin Oakenshield himself, and as they filled the hobbit’s home with boisterous talk of mines and dragons, it became clear that this was no mere social call. It was a conspiracy, and the comfortable Mr. Baggins was to be its fourteenth member.

As the fire died down and the shadows grew long, the dwarves brought forth instruments, and their deep-throated singing filled the hall. They sang of their ancient home within the Lonely Mountain, of the great treasure they had wrought, and of the coming of the dragon Smaug, who had laid their kingdom to ruin and driven them into exile. The song stirred something in Bilbo’s heart; a fierce love of beautiful things made by cunning hands, and a longing to see the great mountains and hear the pine-trees. For a moment, the Tookish part of him wished to wear a sword instead of a walking-stick. This was the first strategic victory of the quest, for the dwarves’ lament had awakened the very spirit Gandalf had sought.

The plan was then laid bare upon the dining table: a map made by Thorin’s grandfather, showing a secret entrance into the Mountain. Here was the strategic heart of the enterprise, a key and a hidden passage that turned a fool’s errand into a plausible venture of burglary. When pressed for his role, poor Bilbo was so overcome by the talk of “a journey from which some of us...may never return” that he let out a shriek and fainted. Hearing the dwarves’ subsequent doubts about his courage—Gloin dismissing him as looking “more like a grocer than a burglar”—piqued his pride. The Took side won. With a sudden burst of spirit, he declared he would join their quest, if he had to “walk from here to the East of East and fight the wild Were-worms in the Last Desert.” And so it was settled. Gandalf’s choice was vindicated, for he knew there was far more to the hobbit than even Bilbo himself had any idea.

2.0 First Steps and a Quarrel with Trolls

The journey began in a flurry, with Bilbo rushing from Bag End without a hat, a walking-stick, or even a pocket-handkerchief. At first, adventures seemed not so bad after all, filled with pony-rides in the May sunshine. But as they passed into the Lone-lands, the roads grew worse and the weather took a nasty turn. A cold, wet evening found the company miserable and, more problematically, without their guide, for Gandalf was nowhere to be seen. This first test revealed a critical weakness: without the wizard’s guidance and foresight, the dwarves were quarrelsome and inept, unable even to start a fire.

It was in this state of despair that their lookout, Balin, spotted a reddish light twinkling through the trees. Suspicious, but driven by the thought of a dry patch and a hot meal, they sent their burglar to investigate. Here was Bilbo’s first true test. Creeping forward with a stealth that was the pride of hobbits, he came upon a startling scene: three very large trolls toasting mutton over a great fire of beech-logs. Listening to their coarse, drawing-room-unfashionable talk, Bilbo knew he ought to return and warn his friends. And yet, he could not bring himself to go back empty-handed. In an act of burglarious pride, he attempted to pick the pocket of one of the trolls, William.

His attempt was an immediate failure. A talking purse in William’s pocket squeaked, “’Ere, ’oo are you?” and Bilbo was caught. His nascent courage gave way to terror, and in his fright he first gave his friends away and then tried to deny their existence, a confusion that only made the trolls more suspicious. Just as they were arguing whether he would make even a mouthful, the dwarves began to arrive, one by one. Pop! went a nasty, smelly sack over each one’s head, and soon the entire company, save Gandalf, lay neatly tied up, awaiting a dreadful fate.

Just as the trolls began a final argument over how to cook their catch—whether to roast them slowly or squash them into jelly—a voice intervened. It was Gandalf, returned in the nick of time. Hidden from view, he masterfully mimicked the trolls’ own voices, setting them to bickering with one another. “Dawn take you all, and be stone to you!” said the wizard’s voice, and just as the first light came over the hill, the trolls were caught by the sun and turned to the very stuff of the mountains. This encounter proved a vital lesson; it demonstrated the company’s utter dependence on Gandalf’s wit and underscored that their greatest weapon was cleverness, not force. The discovery of the trolls’ cave yielded a further, unexpected reward: ancient blades from Gondolin. Thorin claimed Orcrist, the Goblin-cleaver, and Gandalf took up Glamdring, the Foe-hammer. For Bilbo, there was a small knife in a leather sheath—no more than a dagger, but for a hobbit, it was as good as a short sword.

3.0 A Respite in Rivendell

After the grim encounter in the Lone-lands, the company made its way towards the very edge of the Wild, seeking the fair valley of Rivendell where Elrond lived in the Last Homely House. Their path was treacherous, but at last they were guided down a steep zig-zag path into the secret valley, where the air grew warm and the scent of pine-trees filled the air. They were greeted by the laughter and song of elves, a sound that Bilbo found enchanting but which made the dwarves, who do not get on well with such folk, exceedingly uncomfortable.

Their host, Elrond, was as noble as an elf-lord, as strong as a warrior, and as wise as a wizard. In his good house they stayed for fourteen days, their bruises, tempers, and hopes all mended. More than mere rest, however, Rivendell provided two moments of immense strategic importance that would shape the future of the quest. First, Elrond, who knew all about runes of every kind, examined the swords they had taken from the trolls’ lair. “These are old swords, very old swords of the High Elves of the West, my kin,” he said. “This, Thorin, the runes name Orcrist, the Goblin-cleaver...This, Gandalf, was Glamdring, Foe-hammer that the king of Gondolin once wore.” With this knowledge, the company was no longer armed with mere plunder, but with blades of great renown and power against their foes.

The second discovery was of even greater consequence. As Elrond studied Thorin’s map on a midsummer’s eve, the silver crescent of the moon shone through the parchment. “What is this?” he said. “There are moon-letters here.” These were rune-letters so cunningly devised that they could only be seen when a moon of the same shape and season as the day they were written shone behind them. He read the hidden message aloud: “Stand by the grey stone when the thrush knocks, and the setting sun with the last light of Durin’s Day will shine upon the key-hole.”

This revelation was the pivotal moment of their council. The quest was no longer a desperate hope of finding a secret door, but a precise mission against time. They now knew not only where the door was, but the exact and singular conditions under which it could be opened. Armed with this vital intelligence, and with their bags filled with provisions, the company rode away from the Last Homely House with hearts ready for more adventure, their path over the Misty Mountains now clear.

4.0 Over Hill and Under Hill: Captured by Goblins

The journey into the high passes of the Misty Mountains was a hard and dangerous path. The world of safe and comfortable things lay far behind them, and the nights were comfortless and chill. Their true peril began when they were caught in more than a thunderstorm, but a thunder-battle. From their shelter under a hanging rock, Bilbo could see stone-giants hurling rocks at one another for a game, and the wind and rain whipped about them with such fury that Thorin feared they would be “picked up by some giant and kicked sky-high for a football.”

Seeking better shelter, the young dwarves Fili and Kili discovered a dry cave. Though Gandalf was wary, knowing such places were seldom unoccupied, the company and their ponies hurried inside. The respite was short-lived. That night, Bilbo dreamed that a crack in the wall grew wider and wider. He awoke with a horrible start to find his dream was true. The last of the ponies’ tails was just disappearing into a wide passage as great, ugly-looking goblins swarmed out. Before they could react, the dwarves and the hobbit were all grabbed and dragged through the crack. Only Gandalf, wakened by Bilbo’s yell, escaped in a terrific flash of light and a smell like gunpowder.

This capture was a devastating strategic blow. The company lost all of their excellent ponies and the carefully packed supplies from Rivendell, leaving them utterly destitute on the far side of the mountains. They were hauled deep into the earth, down to the great cavern of the Great Goblin. There, before the tremendous goblin and his armed guards, Thorin was interrogated. All might have gone differently had not a goblin guard produced the sword taken from Thorin. The Great Goblin gave a truly awful howl of rage when he saw it, for he knew the blade at once. It was Orcrist, which the goblins called simply Biter, a sword that had slain hundreds of their kind in the ancient wars.

“Murderers and elf-friends!” the Great Goblin shouted, rushing at Thorin. At that very moment, all the lights in the cavern went out. A sword flashed in its own light, slaying the Great Goblin where he stood, and a quiet, fierce voice commanded them to follow. It was Gandalf, who had worked up his best magic in the shadows. He led them on a desperate flight through the tangled tunnels of Goblin-town, with the howls of their pursuers growing ever closer.

5.0 Riddles in the Dark: A Burglar's True Test

In the panicked flight from Goblin-town, the company’s fortune took another dire turn. Dori, who was carrying Bilbo on his back, was grabbed from behind by a goblin. The dwarf fell, and the hobbit rolled off his shoulders, bumped his head on hard rock, and remembered nothing more. This moment of separation, which seemed at first a final disaster, would prove the most pivotal event of the entire quest. When Bilbo opened his eyes, he was utterly alone in a darkness so complete it was as if his eyes were still shut.

Groping about on all fours, his hand met what felt like a tiny ring of cold metal lying on the floor of the tunnel. “It was a turning point in his career, but he did not know it.” He put the ring in his pocket almost without thinking and wandered on, down and down, until he came to the shore of a deep, dark subterranean lake. Here, by the black water, lived old Gollum, a small, slimy creature with two big round pale eyes. Gollum, whose only game was the asking and guessing of riddles, proposed a competition: if he stumped Bilbo, he would eat him; if Bilbo won, he would show him the way out.

Thus began a desperate contest of wits, a sacred game of immense antiquity played in the roots of the mountain with Bilbo’s life as the stake. The hobbit, with his mind uncomfortably on eating, asked of teeth; Gollum, of wind. One after another they traded questions. As the tension mounted, Bilbo found himself struggling for a final riddle. He slapped his pockets, and his hand closed on the ring he had forgotten. “What have I got in my pocket?” he said aloud. Gollum, thinking it a riddle, was frightfully upset, crying “Not fair! not fair!” but Bilbo stuck to his question. Gollum’s three guesses—hands, knife, and string-or-nothing—were all wrong. Bilbo had won.

But Gollum was a miserable, wicked creature. He had no intention of keeping his promise. He slipped away to his island to retrieve his “birthday-present,” a golden ring of power that made the wearer invisible. It was, of course, the very ring now in Bilbo’s pocket. When Gollum returned and discovered it gone, his whisper turned to a piercing shriek of loss, and his suspicion fell at once upon the hobbit. With his pale eyes burning with a green fire, Gollum gave chase. As Bilbo fled, he stumbled, and the ring slipped quietly onto his finger. To his amazement, the raging Gollum passed by without seeing him. The ring was magic. This single, accidental acquisition was a strategic boon beyond measure, an invisible shield that would prove more valuable than all the treasure in the dragon’s hoard.

6.0 Out of the Frying-Pan and Into the Fire

Having escaped Gollum by a leap in the dark, Bilbo followed the wretched creature to a back-door guarded by goblins. Using the ring’s power, he slipped past the guards, bursting the buttons from his waistcoat as he squeezed through a crack in the door, and found himself at last in the open air on the eastern side of the Misty Mountains. Now he faced a crucial test of character: with the ring, he could easily abandon his friends and make his own way home. But it was his duty, he decided, to go back and find them.

He had not gone far when he overheard the dwarves and Gandalf arguing. The dwarves were grumbling about the hobbit, but Gandalf defended him stoutly. “And here’s the burglar!” said Bilbo, stepping into their midst and slipping off the ring. Their surprise and delight were immense, and his reputation as a first-class burglar was sealed. He chose, however, to keep the ring a secret, a decision that gave him a private strength and independence he had not known before.

The reunion was cut short by a long, shuddering howl. They were in the territory of the Wargs, the evil wolves who were allies of the goblins, and a whole pack was gathering. “Up the trees quick!” cried Gandalf, and the company scrambled for their lives. They found themselves treed in the Wargs’ own meeting-place, trapped and surrounded. From the dreadful language of the wolves, Gandalf understood their dire situation: the Wargs were waiting to meet a great goblin-raid planned for that very night.

As the goblins arrived, the situation grew hopeless. They began piling fuel around the trees, intending to burn the company out of their nests. It was a moment of utter despair, with fire below and foes gathering on all sides. Gandalf, preparing to leap down in a final, suicidal blaze of magic, was saved by an intervention that seemed to come from the sky itself. The Lord of the Eagles, his curiosity piqued by the commotion, swept down from the mountain peaks. He seized Gandalf in his talons, and his followers swooped upon the other dwarves, snatching them from the burning treetops. Bilbo, last of all, clung to Dori’s legs as they were borne aloft. They had escaped the goblins and the Wargs, but their flight from the fire marked only the end of the first great stage of their perilous quest.
A Summary of "A Warm Welcome"

Introduction: From Despair to Hope

After the harrowing escape from the dungeons of the Wood-elves, this chapter of the tale marks a profound shift in fortune. The oppressive gloom of Mirkwood at last recedes, giving way to the wide lands of Men and the first glimmers of a rekindled hope. For Thorin and Company, this brief, yet vital, respite upon the shores of the Long Lake is where their journey transforms from a desperate flight into a celebrated quest. It is here that the company’s fortunes truly turn, and the ancient legend of the King under the Mountain is taken up once more, not merely as a whisper among dwarves, but as a song on the lips of Men.

1. Journey's End on the River

So you see, as the company of barrels floated on, the world itself seemed to change. The day grew lighter and warmer, and the river, widening, finally broke free from the claustrophobic grip of the forest. The oppressive canopy gave way to an open sky, and the landscape softened from tangled woods into sprawling marshes and pools dotted with islands. It was an emergence, a slow breath taken after being too long submerged in darkness.

It was then that they saw it. Far away, its dark head shrouded in a torn cloud, loomed the Lonely Mountain. It rose in stark solitude, a grim and ominous peak looking down across the marshes to the forest they had just escaped. For Bilbo, who had come so far and endured so many perils to see it, the sight brought no comfort. Now that the goal was in view, he found that "he did not like the look of it in the least."

As he shivered upon his barrel, Bilbo pieced together scraps of talk from the raft-elves. He learned that the lands had changed much since the days of the dwarves. Great floods and even earthquakes—which some grimly attributed to the dragon—had caused the marshes to spread wide, swallowing up old paths. The Forest River, guarded as it was by the Elvenking, had become the only safe route from the northern skirts of Mirkwood to the plains beyond. Unknown to himself, and by a turn of pure luck, our hobbit had stumbled upon "the only road that was any good."

As the sun began to set, the river took a more southerly course and gathered its waters into a deep, rapid flood. They swept along at great speed until, with a final turn to the east, the Forest River rushed into the Long Lake. The lake was a sight to behold, so vast that its northern end, pointing toward the Mountain, could not be seen at all. From the south came the distant roar of high waterfalls where its doubled waters poured out to unknown lands.

There, shimmering in the twilight not far from the river's mouth, was a sight as strange as any he had heard whispered of in the Elvenking's cellars: a town of Men, built not on the shore, but upon the very surface of the water.

2. The Strange Town of Esgaroth

Now it is a strange thing, but this town was a marvel of human resilience. It was not built upon the shore but right out on the surface of the lake itself, a busy town of wood constructed upon huge piles made from forest trees. This was Esgaroth, or Lake-town, a last bastion of Men who still dared to dwell under the shadow of the dragon-mountain, protected from its wrath by the deep, cool waters of the lake. It remained a hub of trade, persisting despite the desolation that Smaug had wrought upon the lands.

Yet, its bustling quays and great wooden bridge hinted at a grander past. When the waters of the lake sank in a drought, one could still see the "rotting piles of a greater town" along the shores. These were the last remnants of the days of old, when Dale in the north was rich and prosperous, and the dwarf-kings of the Mountain reigned. The memory of that lost glory, however, now lived on only in old songs.

The raft of barrels, guided by boats from the town, was soon drawn from the river’s current and into the calm bay of Lake-town. It was moored near the great bridge that led from the shore, its cargo left to float for the night. The elf and human boatmen, their work done, departed to feast in the town's great hall, leaving the barrels bobbing quietly in the darkness.

They would have been very surprised indeed had they known of the secret activity that was about to unfold by the water’s edge, now that night had fully fallen.

3. Freedom and Misery

In the cold and the dark, with the miserable dwarves packed tightly in their wooden prisons, Bilbo began his clandestine work. It was a risky business, for the slightest noise might bring the notice of the town, but the hobbit knew that this was their last and only chance. He cut the first barrel loose, pushed it to the shore, and opened it. Out crept a groaning Thorin Oakenshield, a wretched and famished figure, so sore and stiff he could hardly stand. He looked, the tale tells us, "like a dog that has been chained and forgotten in a kennel for a week." After a cross exchange where Bilbo reminded him of their urgent need, Thorin agreed to help, and together they set about freeing the others.

The rest of the company was in a sorry state, and their release was a grim and difficult affair.

* Dwalin and Balin: They were among the most unhappy and in no condition to help.
* Bifur and Bofur: While less knocked about, they lay down and refused to do anything.
* Fili and Kili: Being younger and better packed, they emerged "more or less smiling" and offered their willing help. It was Fili who declared, "I hope I never smell the smell of apples again!"
* The Others: Poor fat Bombur was found to be either asleep or senseless, while Dori, Nori, Ori, Oin, and Gloin were so waterlogged they had to be carried ashore like sacks.

When at last the miserable, soaked, and bruised company was gathered on the bank, Thorin spoke. He offered a grudging thanks to Bilbo before turning to the pressing matter at hand. "In the meanwhile," he asked, "what next?" The answer was clear, and leaving the weakest of the company behind, Thorin, Fili, Kili, and Bilbo set off towards the lights of the guard-hut on the bridge.

4. The Return of the King

This was the moment upon which the quest would turn. Thorin, despite his tattered clothes and draggled hood, was about to reclaim his royal heritage with a declaration so bold it would stir the hearts and memories of an entire town. He was no longer a mere fugitive, but a king returning to his own.

The guards at the head of the bridge were keeping no careful watch. They sat drinking and laughing by a fire, and their astonishment was immense when Thorin Oakenshield himself stepped into their midst. Before they could fully grasp their weapons, he announced his presence in a voice full of ancient authority:

"Thorin son of Thrain son of Thror King under the Mountain! I have come back. I wish to see the Master of your town!"

A "tremendous excitement" immediately erupted. The captain of the guard, coming forward, challenged them, but an impatient Fili cut him short. "We are worn and famished after our long road," he declared. "Now make haste and let us have no more words." Faced with such royal and impatient command, there was nothing for the guards to do but obey. And so, with an escort of six guards, the party was marched across the great bridge, through the gates, and into the market-place, moving ever closer to the great hall where the Master of the town was feasting.

5. A Tumultuous Welcome

The reception in Lake-town was a maelstrom of conflicting interests. The Master, a pragmatic man concerned with trade and tolls, was deeply skeptical. The raft-elves, surprised to see their escaped prisoners, were quick to denounce them. But against these forces of doubt and accusation rose a tide of overwhelming enthusiasm from the common people, whose hearts were stirred by the legends they had long sung.

In the Master's hall, Thorin repeated his momentous declaration. The elves immediately accused the dwarves of being escaped prisoners, but Thorin powerfully rebutted their claims. "Nor is this town in the Wood-elves' realm," he declared. "I speak to the Master of the town of the Men of the Lake, not to the raft-men of the king." As news of the King's return spread like fire through the town, a great clamour arose. The people, swept up in a sudden joy, began to sing the old songs of the Mountain:

The King beneath the mountains, The King of carven stone, The lord of silver fountains Shall come into his own!

The Master of Lake-town was a shrewd politician. Seeing the tide of public opinion turn so fiercely, he wisely chose to "obey the general clamour." He gave up his own great chair to Thorin and welcomed the entire company with feigned enthusiasm, settling the matter for the moment.

For two weeks, the dwarves were "doctored and fed and housed and pampered." They quickly regained their strength and their proud demeanor, while poor Bilbo, having been soaked to the bone, was left to nurse a "shocking cold."

6. Departure for the Mountain

While the company recuperated, news of their quest spread far and wide, and the great powers of the region began to align themselves. The end of this final respite was drawing near, and the time for the ultimate confrontation was at hand.

The Elvenking, upon hearing the news, was filled with suspicion. He believed the dwarves would come to a bad end and strongly suspected them of "attempted burglary." He was, however, a wise elf and made a shrewd resolution, thinking to himself: "No treasure will come back through Mirkwood without my having something to say in the matter." He sent his spies to watch the shores of the lake and waited.

Thorin, for his part, also acted wisely. He knew he must capitalize on the town's enthusiasm before it could cool and secure their aid for the final leg of the journey. The Master of the town, meanwhile, played a double game. In his heart, he hoped Smaug would make short work of the dwarves, thus ridding him of a costly and disruptive party. Publicly, however, he pledged his aid, trusting in their "gratitude when your kingdom is regained."

And so, one autumn day, three large boats set out from Lake-town. The quays were filled with people who cheered and sang as the company departed. Amidst songs and cheers, the white oars dipped and splashed, carrying the company north up the lake and onto the last stage of their long journey. But in the heart of all the celebration, one small figure felt only a deep and growing dread. For as the chronicle tells, "The only person thoroughly unhappy was Bilbo."
